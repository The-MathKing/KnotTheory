=============================================================================
ABSTRACT  (178 words)
=============================================================================

Rashidi, Shajareh Poursalavati and Tavakkoli, in their Theorem 3.6, state that
Z(P(n,3)) = 8 for all n at least 12. Krishnan showed that this fails at n = 12,
where Z(P(12,3)) = 7, computed Z(P(n,3)) exhaustively for n from 7 to 20, and
conjectured that Z(P(n,3)) = 8 for every n at least 13; he identified the
missing ingredient as a lower-bound proof valid for all large n. We supply it
and prove the conjecture.

The method is a reduction: eliminating the inner coordinates of any matrix
carrying the pattern of P(n,3) leaves a single linear recurrence of order 8, so
the nullity equals the dimension of the fixed space of the monodromy T, and is
at most 8, with equality exactly when T is the identity.

We then build matrices attaining 8 by concatenating tiles with a fixed docking
pattern, whose transfer products depend only on their interiors; tiles of every
length from 21 up to but not including 42 give every n at least 21 at once.
Each tile is certified by a Krawczyk contraction evaluated in exact rational
arithmetic.

Together with per-n certificates for n from 17 to 20 and exhaustive search for
n from 13 to 16, this gives Z(P(n,3)) = 8 for every n at least 13, and 13 is
optimal since Z(P(11,3)) = Z(P(12,3)) = 7.


=============================================================================
SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION  (about 420 words)
=============================================================================

Let G be a graph whose vertices are each coloured black or white. The colour
change rule is: if a black vertex has exactly one white neighbour, that
neighbour becomes black. A set S of vertices is zero forcing if colouring S
black and applying the rule until it stalls colours every vertex; the zero
forcing number Z(G) is the least size of such a set. Writing M(G) for the
maximum nullity over symmetric real matrices whose off-diagonal support is
exactly the edge set of G, one has M(G) at most Z(G).

The generalized Petersen graph P(n,k), for n at least 3 and k between 1 and
n/2, has vertices u_0 through u_{n-1} and v_0 through v_{n-1}, with edges
joining u_i to u_{i+1}, v_i to v_{i+k}, and u_i to v_i, indices taken modulo n.
It is cubic.

Rashidi, Shajareh Poursalavati and Tavakkoli state in their Theorem 3.6 that
Z(P(n,3)) = 8 for every n at least 12. Krishnan showed that the statement fails
at n = 12, exhibiting an explicit seven-element zero forcing set for P(12,3)
and confirming by exhaustive search that no six-element set forces; he located
the gap in the published argument, whose case analysis never excludes
seven-element sets. He computed Z(P(n,3)) exhaustively for n from 7 to 20,
observed that the values stabilise at 8 from n = 13 onward, and recorded as
Conjecture 5 that Z(P(n,3)) = 8 for every n at least 13, noting that the
obstacle is a matching lower bound valid for all n: the upper bound is
available for every n at least 9, and only finitely many lower bounds can come
from computation.

[DISPLAY: the main theorem. Z(P(n,3)) = 8 for every n at least 13, and 13 is
optimal, since Z(P(11,3)) = Z(P(12,3)) = 7. Moreover Z = M = 8 for every n at
least 17.]

This is Conjecture 5 of Krishnan.

The upper bound Z(P(n,3)) at most 8 for n at least 9 is a rotation argument and
is not new. The content is the lower bound, and all of it is in Sections 3
through 5. Section 3 reduces the kernel of any matrix carrying the pattern of
P(n,3) to the periodic solutions of a linear recurrence of order 8, which
bounds the nullity by 8 and identifies exactly when the bound is attained: when
the monodromy of the recurrence is the identity. Section 4 makes that condition
achievable for every large n simultaneously, by assembling the weight sequence
out of tiles whose transfer products multiply independently. Section 5
certifies the tiles. Section 6 assembles the proof.


=============================================================================
SECTION 2 — SETUP  (about 190 words)
=============================================================================

Fix n and k with n at least 2k+3. Let A be a real matrix indexed by the
vertices of P(n,k), with a nonzero entry in position xy if and only if xy is an
edge, for x not equal to y; the diagonal is unrestricted. We do not assume A is
symmetric.

[DISPLAY: the weight names. b_i and b'_i for the two directions of the outer
edge, c_i and c'_i for the spoke, e_i and e'_i for the inner edge, with
diagonal entries a_i at u_i and d_i at v_i. All of these are nonzero.]

Symmetry is not needed for the bound that the nullity of A is at most Z(G). It
is enough that every edge carries a nonzero entry in each direction. Indeed, if
Ax = 0 and x vanishes on a set B, and some vertex u in B has exactly one
neighbour w outside B, then the row of Ax = 0 at u has every term vanishing
except the one involving x_w, whose coefficient is nonzero; hence x_w = 0.
Induction along a forcing process shows that restriction to S is injective on
the kernel of A for every zero forcing set S, so the nullity of A is at most
Z(G).

SECTION 3 — THE REDUCTION  (about 330 words)
=============================================================================

The outer and inner equations of the kernel of A can be combined into one
recurrence along the outer cycle. Specialised to k = 3 the recurrence has order
8, which is exactly the bound to be matched.

[DISPLAY: Theorem (Reduction). For n at least 2k+3, define L x at position i by
solving the outer equation for the inner coordinate. Then the map sending x to
the pair (x, Lx) is a linear bijection from the solutions of a nine-term
recurrence onto the kernel of A. The two extreme coefficients, at offsets
plus and minus (k+1), are given explicitly and are nonzero. Consequently the
nullity of A equals the dimension of the fixed space of the monodromy T, is at
most 2k+2, and equals 2k+2 exactly when T is the identity.]

Proof. Each spoke weight is nonzero, so the equation of the kernel at u_i
solves uniquely for the inner coordinate at i, a three-term expression in x.
Substituting into the equation at v_i gives the stated relation: the three
substituted terms contribute three index triples, and the two extreme
coefficients are as stated, both nonzero because the outer, spoke and inner
weights are. Conversely any x satisfying all the relations yields a kernel
vector.

Because both end coefficients are nonzero, the relation is a recurrence of
order exactly 2k+2, solvable forward and backward, so its solutions on the
integers form a space of dimension 2k+2 on which the monodromy acts, and the
n-periodic solutions are exactly the fixed vectors of the monodromy. Hence the
nullity of A is the dimension of that fixed space, which is at most 2k+2 and
equals 2k+2 precisely when the monodromy is the identity.

The hypothesis that n is at least 2k+3 is needed: for n equal to 2k+1 and
2k+2 the index sets above collide modulo n, the extreme coefficients are no
longer given by the displayed formulas, and the order of the recurrence can
drop.

[DISPLAY: Corollary. M(P(n,3)) is at most 8 for n at least 9.]

For k = 3 the recurrence has order 8, and 8 is also the size of the forcing set
produced by the rotation argument that gives the upper bound. The ceiling of
the matrix method and the upper bound it is trying to match therefore coincide,
with no slack on either side: a matrix on the pattern of P(n,3) with nullity 8
exists if and only if the monodromy of its reduced recurrence is the identity,
and such a matrix proves the lower bound.

SECTION 4 — TILES  (about 310 words)
=============================================================================

Fix the docking pattern: at a position, the outer, spoke and inner weights are
all 1 and the two diagonal entries are 0. A tile of length l is a block of l
consecutive positions whose first k+1 and last k+1 positions carry the docking
pattern; the remaining positions are its interior. The tile product is the
product of the transfer matrices over the tile's positions.

[DISPLAY: Lemma (Tile independence). If the weight sequence is a concatenation
of tiles sharing one docking pattern, each tile product depends only on that
tile's interior weights, and the monodromy is the product of the tile
products.]

Proof. By the reduction theorem, each transfer matrix is determined by the
coefficients of one reduced equation, which involve weights only at indices in
a window of width 2k+2 around its position. If the tile occupies a block and
the position lies in that block, the window lies within the block extended by
k+1 on each side. The part below the block lies in the preceding tile's final
k+1 positions and the part above lies in the following tile's first k+1
positions; both carry the docking pattern, as do the tile's own first and last
k+1 positions. So every weight the transfer matrix sees is either docking or
interior to this tile. The second statement is associativity of the monodromy
product regrouped along the tiles.

[DISPLAY: Theorem (Tiling). Fix k and L at least 2(k+1). Suppose that for every
length from L up to but not including 2L there are interior weights, all
required entries nonzero, making the tile product equal to the identity. Then
Z(P(n,k)) = M(P(n,k)) = 2k+2 for every n at least L.]

Proof. Every n at least L is a sum of integers from that range: if n is below
2L take the single term n; if n is at least 2L take a term L and recurse on
n minus L. Concatenating the corresponding tiles gives a weight sequence whose
monodromy is, by the lemma, the product of the tile products, hence the
identity. All required weights are nonzero, so the matrix carries the pattern
of P(n,k), and the reduction theorem gives nullity 2k+2. With the inequality
from Section 2 and the rotation bound, both quantities equal 2k+2.

Using a full interval is what makes the conclusion gapless. Two tile lengths
that are coprime would reach only sums of multiples of the two, leaving every n
below a certain product uncovered, which for the smallest solvable lengths here
is already several hundred. The hypothesis is a finite list of independent
finite problems.

SECTION 5 — CERTIFICATION  (about 330 words)
=============================================================================

For k = 3 we take L = 21 and exhibit identity tiles of every length from 21 to
43 inclusive, which contains the required range; that is 23 tiles.

Certifying that the tile product is the identity directly would mean interval
arithmetic on a rational map. It is unnecessary. By the reduction theorem
applied to the cyclic matrix built from a single tile, the monodromy of that
matrix is the tile product, so the tile product is the identity if and only if
that matrix has nullity 2k+2, and the latter is a statement about a bilinear
system. Writing K for a matrix whose columns span a candidate kernel, in graph
form, the condition is that A times K vanishes, and its Jacobian is affine in
the unknowns.

The square subsystem is chosen structurally rather than as a maximal-rank
selection: the product of K transpose, A and K is symmetric, and its
antisymmetric part accounts for exactly 28 dependencies among the equations, so
removing those leaves a system equivalent to the original rather than a weaker
consequence of it.

Each tile is then certified by a Krawczyk contraction.

[DISPLAY: the two Krawczyk inequalities, in terms of an approximate inverse Y
of the square Jacobian, a Lipschitz bound for the Jacobian, and a box radius.
Together they establish that a true real solution exists in the box.]

Every quantity in these inequalities is evaluated in exact rational arithmetic:
the box centre is dyadic, the system is bilinear with integer coefficients so
its value and Jacobian at that centre are exactly rational, and the approximate
inverse is arbitrary, so rounding it to a rational costs nothing. Across all 23
tiles the contraction constant is at most 1.0 times 10 to the minus 9, every
certified matrix has nullity exactly 8, and every docking weight equals its
prescribed integer. No step rests on a floating-point nullity computation.

The same machinery, applied per n rather than per tile, certifies that Z and M
both equal 8 for every n from 17 to 33, seventeen consecutive values including
the primes 17, 19, 23, 29 and 31.


=============================================================================
SECTION 6 — PROOF OF THE THEOREM  (about 190 words)
=============================================================================

Proof. The upper bound holds for every n at least 9 by the rotation argument.

For n at least 21: Section 5 provides certified identity tiles of every length
in the required range, so the tiling theorem with L = 21 gives Z = M = 8.

For n from 17 to 20: these are covered by the per-n certificates of Section 5,
which give Z = M = 8 on the range 17 to 33.

For n from 13 to 16: exhaustive search over vertex subsets shows that no zero
forcing set of size 7 exists, so Z = 8 by the upper bound. These four values
give Z only; we do not claim that M = 8 there.

Optimality of 13: exhaustive search gives Z(P(11,3)) = Z(P(12,3)) = 7.

Remark. The sequence of values for n from 7 to 14 is 6, 6, 6, 8, 7, 7, 8, 8. It
is not monotone, and the bound 8 is first attained at n = 10, well before the
threshold 13 beyond which it holds always. The two are different quantities.

Remark. By the reduction theorem the lower bound is attained exactly when a
monodromy equals the identity, a condition with no arithmetic in it. This is
why the argument reaches every large n, including primes, rather than a
congruence class.
