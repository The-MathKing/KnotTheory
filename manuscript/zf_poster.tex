\shead{1. The problem}
For a graph $G$, colour a set $S$ of vertices. If a coloured vertex has exactly
one uncoloured neighbour, that neighbour becomes coloured; repeat. $Z(G)$ is the
least $|S|$ that eventually colours everything. $Z$ bounds the \emph{maximum
nullity} $M(G)$, the largest null space among symmetric matrices whose
off-diagonal support is exactly $E(G)$:
\[ M(G)\le Z(G). \]
The \emph{generalized Petersen graph} $P(n,k)$ has outer cycle $u_iu_{i+1}$,
inner edges $v_iv_{i+k}$ and spokes $u_iv_i$: cubic on $2n$ vertices, with the
rotation $\rho:i\mapsto i+1$.

More generally $P(n,k)$ is a \emph{cyclic cover}: a base graph with edge
voltages in $\mathbb Z_n$, lifted to $V(B)\times\mathbb Z_n$. Two vertices,
loops of voltage $1$ and $k$, a spoke of voltage $0$. Everything below is really
about covers.

\shead{2. What was open}
Rashidi, Shajareh Poursalavati and Tavakkoli~[1] proved $Z(P(n,k))\le2k+2$ and
$Z(P(n,2))=6$ for $n\ge10$. Their Theorem 3.6, claiming $Z(P(n,3))=8$ for
$n\ge12$, is \emph{false}: Krishnan~[2] exhibits $Z(P(12,3))=7$, the flaw being
a case analysis that never excludes $7$-vertex sets. That July 2026 note closes
by recording as open

\vspace{0.4ex}
\hspace{1em}\emph{the stabilization threshold, and a rigorous lower bound for
$k\ge4$.}
\vspace{0.4ex}

Lower bounds are the hard direction: an upper bound exhibits one forcing set, a
lower bound must exclude \emph{every} smaller one.

\shead{3. Why the standard tools fail}
A set is forcing iff it meets every \emph{fort}. All three standard
consequences degrade as $k$ grows, and are insufficient \emph{in principle}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2ex}
\item disjoint forts: at most \emph{two} exist in any case tested;
\item fort LP: integrality gap $\approx2$ ($\mathrm{LP}=3.21$ against $Z=10$ for
      $P(18,4)$), which also explains why fort solvers scale poorly here;
\item spectral: Hoffman-type bounds give $1.2$--$2.9$ against true $Z$ of
      $6$--$12$.
\end{itemize}
This is what motivated changing the \emph{category} of argument, from
combinatorial to matrix-analytic.

\shead{4. The ceiling}
Every spoke weight $c_i$ is nonzero, so the kernel equation at $u_i$ solves for
$y_i=-(b'_{i-1}x_{i-1}+a_ix_i+b_ix_{i+1})/c_i$. Substituting into the equation
at $v_i$ eliminates $y$ entirely and leaves \emph{one scalar recurrence} in $x$,
supported on nine indices, with both end coefficients
$\gamma_{i,\pm(k+1)}\neq0$.

\thmn{Theorem}{Let $n\ge2k+3$ and let $A$ have off-diagonal support exactly
$E(P(n,k))$ --- symmetric \emph{or not}. Then
$\operatorname{null}A=\dim\ker(T-I)\le 2k+2$, where $T$ is the monodromy of that
order-$(2k+2)$ recurrence, with equality \emph{iff} $T=I$.}

The hypothesis is sharp: at $n=2k+1,2k+2$ the offsets $\pm(k+1)$ collide modulo
$n$ and the order drops.

\headline{The order $2k+2$ is exactly the size of the forcing set the rotation
bootstrap produces. \textbf{The ceiling of the matrix method equals the upper
bound it is trying to match} --- so either the method settles $Z$ completely, or
nothing does.}

comes from.}{1.0}

\shead{5. Reaching the ceiling: the symbol}
For $A$ invariant under $\rho$ the Fourier blocks are
$M_m=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a+s_m&c\\ c&d+et_m\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
$s_m=2\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n}$. Since $t=L_k(s)$ exactly, with $L_k$ the monic
integer polynomial satisfying $L_k(z+z^{-1})=z^k+z^{-k}$,
\[
 \operatorname{null}A=\#\{m: F(s_m)=0\},\quad
 F(s)=(s+a)L_k(s)+\alpha s+\beta,
\]
monic of degree $k+1$ but carrying \emph{exactly three} free parameters, for
every $k$. Three roots may be prescribed and no more --- but prescribing three
\emph{determines the other $k-2$}, and if $F$ is rational its roots form Galois
orbits, which stay inside the grid $\Gamma_n=\{2\cos(2\pi m/n)\}$. One rational
$F$ places all $k+1$ roots at once. Taking $F=\prod_{d\in D}\Psi_d$, a product
of minimal polynomials of $2\cos(2\pi/d)$, gives \emph{integer} certificates.

\thmn{Theorem}{Realisability is a factorisation: $c^2=a\alpha-\beta=0$ if and
only if $F=(s+a)\bigl(L_k(s)+t\bigr)$. Over $\mathbb Q$, Niven's theorem forces
$a,t\in\{0,\pm1,\pm2\}$, so at most $25$ degenerate symbols exist for each $k$.}

Only $c^2=0$ is fatal: $c^2$ is the \emph{product} of the two spoke weights, so
$c^2<0$ is realisable once they may differ. Imposing $c^2>0$ instead --- our own
earlier error --- wrongly ruled out $k=3$ and $k=7$.

\shead{6. Why congruence conditions are forced}
Every certificate above holds only for $n$ in a divisibility class. That is not
a shortfall in the search:

\thmn{Theorem}{A period-one symbol is a \emph{fixed} polynomial; its roots are
fixed algebraic numbers, and $2\cos(2\pi j/d)\in\Gamma_n$ iff $d\mid n$. So one
such matrix settles exactly $\{n:\operatorname{lcm}(D)\mid n\}$ --- a single
class, containing \textbf{at most one prime}. For $k\ge3$ every symbol obeys
$e_2=-k$, a Conway--Jones relation on the grid.}

For $k=2$ that coefficient is free, \emph{which is exactly why $k=2$ alone was
ever general in $n$}. Escaping the classes therefore requires abandoning
rotation-invariance altogether --- and Theorem 1 characterises the ceiling for
\emph{every} matrix on the pattern by $T=I$, a condition with no arithmetic in
it.

\shead{7. Tiling: all large $n$}
Build the weight sequence out of pieces. Pieces do not compose naively, since
$T_i$ depends on weights within $2k+2$ of position $i$; so pin a \emph{docking
pattern} on the first and last $k+1$ positions of every tile.

\thmn{Lemma}{Each tile's transfer product then depends only on its own interior,
and $T=\prod T_{\mathrm{tile}}$.}

\thmn{Theorem}{If identity tiles exist for every length in $[L,2L)$, then
$Z(P(n,k))=M(P(n,k))=2k+2$ for \emph{every} $n\ge L$.}

\proofn{Every $n\ge L$ is a sum of lengths drawn from $[L,2L)$: take one tile if
$n<2L$, else remove a tile of length $L$ and recurse. The monodromy is the
product of the tile products, hence $I$; apply Theorem 1.}

A full interval is what makes this gapless. Two coprime lengths $a,b$ would give
only $n=ia+jb$, leaving every $n<(a-1)(b-1)$ uncovered --- several hundred at
the smallest solvable lengths.

\figrow{figures/fig_tiling.pdf}{Figure 2. Tiles compose because their ends are
pinned; all large $n$ becomes a finite list of finite problems.}{1.0}

\shead{8. Certifying a tile}
$T_{\mathrm{tile}}=I$ is \emph{rational} in the weights, so a Krawczyk bound on
it would need interval matrix products. It is unnecessary: by Theorem 1 the
monodromy of the \emph{one-tile} cyclic matrix \emph{is} the tile product, so
\[
 T_{\mathrm{tile}}=I \iff \operatorname{null}A_{\text{one tile}}=2k+2,
\]
and a nullity is certified by the \textbf{bilinear} system $A(w)K(X)=0$, whose
Jacobian is affine and whose Lipschitz constant is an explicit integer.

$AK=0$ has rank $2nr-\binom r2$, and the deficiency is structural:
$K^{\!\top}\!AK$ is symmetric because $A$ is, and $\binom r2$ is its
antisymmetric part. Choosing the square subsystem \emph{structurally} ---
non-pivot rows plus the upper triangle of the pivot block --- makes it
\emph{equivalent} to $AK=0$; choosing it by pivoting leaves $\binom r2$
equations unproved and the Krawczyk test still passes. Those equations fall to
$10^{-61}$ without being solved for.

\shead{9. Results}
\refstepcounter{res}
\headline{\textbf{Theorem \theres.} \emph{With no congruence condition:}
\vspace{-0.8ex}
\[\begin{array}{ll}
 Z(P(n,2))=M(P(n,2))=6  & \text{for all } n\ge9,\ n\ne10,\\[1pt]
 Z(P(n,3))=M(P(n,3))=8  & \text{for all } n\ge17,\\[1pt]
 Z(P(n,4))=M(P(n,4))=10 & \text{for all } n\ge30 .
\end{array}\]
\vspace{-2.2ex}}

\thmn{Theorem}{$Z(P(n,2))$ and $Z(P(n,3))$ are determined for \emph{every}
admissible $n$. In particular $Z(P(n,3))=8$ for every $n\ge13$, and $13$ is
optimal: $Z(P(11,3))=Z(P(12,3))=7$.}

This determines the \textbf{stabilization threshold for $k=3$} --- one of the
two problems [2] records as open --- and the exact values for $k=4$ answer the
other. The sequence is genuinely non-monotone below the threshold:
$Z(P(n,3))=6,6,6,8,7,7,8,8,\dots$ for $n=7,\dots,14$.

\figrow{figures/fig_coverage.pdf}{Figure 3. Sparse congruence classes (dots)
against every $n$ past a threshold (bars).}{1.0}

\shead{10. How each is proved}
$k=2$: a closed form. Matching coefficients gives
$c^2=-(s_1+s_2)(s_1+s_3)(s_2+s_3)$, so $c^2=0$ iff two roots are antipodal; the
three most negative grid values make it positive for all $n\ge9$, $n\ne10$.

$k=3,4$: the tiling theorem with $L=21$ and $L=31$, from \textbf{54 certified
identity tiles} covering $[21,43]$ and $[31,61]$. Below $L$, $k=3$ is closed by
$17$ consecutive individually certified values, $17\le n\le33$.

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}clccc@{}}
\toprule
$k$ & settles & $L$ & tiles & $\max\alpha$\\
\midrule
$2$ & $n\ge9$, $n\ne10$ & --- & closed form & ---\\
$3$ & $n\ge17$ & $21$ & $23$ & $2.1\times10^{-10}$\\
$4$ & $n\ge30$ & $30$ & $32$ & $3.8\times10^{-10}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Each tile is an independent Krawczyk contraction with its own unknown count,
$\|Y\|_\infty$ and residual; nothing is copied between them. At $k=4$,
$\ell=61$: $1425$ unknowns, $1220$ equations of which $1175$ are structural and
$45=\binom{10}2$ dependent, high-precision residual $4.7\times10^{-61}$.

\shead{11. Verification}
One script re-derives everything: \texttt{verify\_all.py}, \textbf{69 checks,
all passing} --- the realisability theorem symbolically for $k=2..10$, every
integer certificate, all $54$ tiles re-evaluated for nullity \emph{and} exact
docking, and the claim that every $n$ in range is a sum of certified lengths.
The structural theorem was checked on $189$ random matrices over $63$ pairs
$(n,k)$. The exhaustive $Z$ solver reproduced every published value first,
including the corrected $Z(P(12,3))=7$.

The $n=17$ certificate was found three independent ways --- minimising
$\|T-I\|$, an alternating solve, a gauge-fixed search --- all yielding a
certified matrix. Stage 1 is only a search; stages 2--3 are the proof.

\headline{\textbf{Why a failed search proves nothing here.} Every symmetric
matrix on the pattern is gauge-equivalent to one in the slice we search, so a
failure inside it measures effort, not impossibility. $P(18,3)$ and the tile
$\ell=21$ were both reported as failures and both succeeded once the restart
budget was raised. \emph{Working rule: quote the residual and the budget, never
just ``failed''.}}

\shead{12. Limits, and what is not proved}
$k\ge5$ is \emph{not} settled: tiles exist at $k=5$ from $\ell\approx54$, but
$[54,108)$ needs $54$ of them and is incomplete. $7\le n\le12$ at $k=3$ and
$9\le n\le30$ at $k=4$ are open. The $k\ge6$ congruence-class values remain
numerical. The rank law is verified over a range, not proved.

We first searched $k=5$ over $41\le\ell\le45$, the parameter count's bound,
found nothing, and later found tiles starting near $54$: the count is a lower
bound, \emph{not} a predictor of where tiles begin, and we cannot explain the
gap. Among claims we have withdrawn: an unconditional cover bound
($Z(P(8,2))=5$ refutes it); the threshold $(2k+2)^2/5$; $N(k)=O(k)$; and an
eigenvalue search whose negative results we retracted after it reported nullity
$7$ at $P(20,3)$ where $8$ is attained.
