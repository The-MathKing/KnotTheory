\begin{abstract}
After four consecutive candidate results in low-dimensional topology turned
out to be already known (\texttt{manuscript/log.tex}, Log Entries 17, 19, 23),
this log records a deliberate pivot to combinatorial game theory and its
outcome: a fifth miss, and the sharpest one so far. The octal game
\texttt{0.45} was selected as a target on the belief that its conjectured
period-$20$ nim-sequence was unproved. It is not. A.~Flammenkamp's
octal-game database lists \texttt{.45} in its table of \emph{Nontrivial
Octal-Games with known Structure} with a ``solved'' date of \textbf{1956} ---
that is, the game was settled by Guy and Smith in the paper that introduced
the notation. Independently of that citation, the Guy--Smith periodicity
theorem closes the game in half a page and requires the nim-sequence to be
checked only as far as $n=2p+2\ell+t=1038$; that argument is re-derived from
scratch and verified here. What survives is a fully validated Sprague--Grundy
engine, which reproduces all four of Flammenkamp's tabulated statistics for
\texttt{.45} exactly, and a reading rule stated in Entry~5: the failure this
time was not inferring openness from silence, but misreading a primary source
that gave the answer in a column I did not parse. Both the wrong claim and its
correction are left in place below, in order.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Entry 1: Why This Problem}
\textbf{Date:} September 23, 2026 \\
After the knot-theory audit's fourth consecutive miss, a different area
was sought, following the standard applied throughout: find a specific,
well-defined, checkably-open target before committing effort, rather
than assume openness.

\textbf{The area.} Octal games (Guy--Smith notation \texttt{0.}$d_1d_2d_3\ldots$)
are take-and-break Nim variants; whether every finite one has an eventually
periodic nim-sequence is a genuine open conjecture in the field, and
Flammenkamp's page does record $65$ unsettled $2$-place and $8$ unsettled
$3$-place octal games. That much was right.

\textbf{The candidate.} \texttt{0.45}: remove $1$ token from a heap only by
splitting the remainder into two nonempty heaps (needs heap $\ge3$); remove
$2$ tokens either to end the heap (heap $=2$) or by splitting the remainder
(needs heap $\ge4$). Its database entry gives preperiod $498$, period $20$ ---
by a wide margin the smallest such pair among the small-code games examined
(\texttt{.356}: 7315/142; \texttt{.156}: 3479/349; \texttt{.644}: 3256/442).
\texttt{0.45} is not among the four games (\texttt{.127}, \texttt{.16},
\texttt{.376}, \texttt{.56}) proved periodic by Gangolli--Plambeck, and I read
its absence from that list, plus the field's ``only about twenty proved
periodic'' framing, as evidence that it was unproved. \emph{This inference was
wrong; see Entry~5.}

\section*{Entry 2: A Validated Engine}
\textbf{Date:} September 23, 2026 \quad
\textbf{Command:} \texttt{python src/octal\_games/grundy.py} \\
\texttt{src/octal\_games/grundy.py} computes Sprague--Grundy sequences for
arbitrary octal codes from scratch (a direct mex computation, no
special-casing). This part of the work stands, and is the most reusable thing
the investigation produced.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Kayles} (\texttt{0.77}): the engine's own search finds preperiod
$71$, period $12$ --- an exact match to R.~K.~Guy's 1949 hand-proved result.
\item \textbf{Four period cross-checks}: \texttt{0.45} (498, 20),
\texttt{0.356} (7315, 142), \texttt{0.156} (3479, 349), \texttt{0.644}
(3256, 442), all reproduced exactly.
\item \textbf{Four statistic cross-checks on \texttt{.45}}, added during the
audit and stronger than the period check alone, since they test four
independent columns of the same database row rather than one:
\end{itemize}

Flammenkamp column & his value & computed & \\

\section*{Entry 3: Three Structural Findings}
\textbf{Date:} September 23, 2026 \\
Computed to $N=60{,}000$, later extended to $N=200{,}000$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Boundedness.} $G(n)\in S=\{1,2,4,7,8\}$ for all
$199\le n\le 200{,}000$, with zero exceptions; below $199$ there are
exactly $11$ exceptions, namely
$n=0,1,6,11,19,31,$ $78,88,98,106,198$.
\item \textbf{Stabilization.} The mex-determining reachable set at $n$ draws
in principle on all splits $a+b=n-1$ or $n-2$, a range growing without bound.
Restricted to the values $\{0,\dots,8\}$ that can affect the mex, it is
determined by the first $A$ split terms with $A\le98$ across $416$ sampled
$n\in[500,60{,}000]$.
\item \textbf{Closure.} Combining 1 and 2, the mex-relevant reachable set, and
hence $G(n)$, depends only on $n\bmod 20$ once the relevant window lies past
$n=199$.
\end{enumerate}
Finding 2's bound is worth one remark, because it is the one place where my
own computation and the database met independently: the widest offset the
recursion actually needs is $198+t=200$, and $200$ is exactly the value in
Flammenkamp's \texttt{last+t} column. I computed that number without knowing
what the column meant.

\section*{Entry 4: The Correct Argument}
\textbf{Date:} September 25, 2026 \\
\textbf{Command:}
{\small\texttt{python verification/prove\_octal\_45\_periodicity.py}} \\
An earlier version of this log built a bespoke proof on Entry~3: a bounded
value set $S$, the ten-index exceptional set
$R=\{1,6,11,19,31,$ $78,88,98,106,198\}$, an XOR-closure lemma
($x,y\in S$ and $x\oplus y\le8$ imply $x\oplus y\in C=\{0,3,5,6\}$), and a
reachable-set formula proved by strong induction. That argument had one
acknowledged gap: that the bulk contribution realises \emph{all} of $C$
and not merely a subset of it, which I could not establish without
assuming the very periodicity being proved. The whole apparatus is
unnecessary. None of $S$, $C$, $R$ or boundedness is needed.

\begin{theorem}[Guy--Smith, specialised to \texttt{0.45}]
Let $G$ be the nim-sequence of \texttt{0.45}, let $\ell=20$, $p=498$, and let
$t=2$ be the index of the last nonzero code digit. If $G(m)=G(m-\ell)$ for
every $m$ with $p+\ell\le m\le 2p+2\ell+t$, then $G(n)=G(n-\ell)$ for all
$n\ge p+\ell$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $n\ge4$ the game's moves give
$\mathrm{Reach}(n)=\{G(a)\oplus G(b): a+b=n-i,\ a,b\ge1,\ i\in\{1,2\}\}$ and
$G(n)=\operatorname{mex}\mathrm{Reach}(n)$; there is no move leaving exactly
one heap, and the move that empties the heap applies only at $n=2$.
Induct on $n>2p+2\ell+t$, assuming $G(m)=G(m-\ell)$ for all $p+\ell\le m<n$.

\emph{$\mathrm{Reach}(n)\subseteq\mathrm{Reach}(n-\ell)$.} Take $a+b=n-i$ with
$a\le b$, $a,b\ge1$. Then $b\ge(n-i)/2\ge(n-t)/2\ge p+\ell$ and
$b\le n-i-1<n$, so the hypothesis gives $G(b)=G(b-\ell)$, and $b-\ell\ge p\ge1$.
Hence $\{a,b-\ell\}$ is a pair of nonempty heaps summing to $(n-\ell)-i$ with
the same XOR. Heaps are unordered, so no condition relating $a$ and $b-\ell$
is required --- this is the step my earlier write-up split into two cases
unnecessarily.

\emph{$\mathrm{Reach}(n-\ell)\subseteq\mathrm{Reach}(n)$.} Take
$a+b=(n-\ell)-i$ with $a\le b$. Then $b\ge(n-\ell-i)/2\ge p$ and
$b+\ell\le n-i-1<n$, so $G(b+\ell)=G(b)$ and $\{a,b+\ell\}$ sums to $n-i$
with the same XOR.

Neither $n$ nor $n-\ell$ equals $2$ in this range, so the terminal move
contributes to both or neither. Equal reachable sets have equal mex, so
$G(n)=G(n-\ell)$.
\end{proof}

With $p=498$, $\ell=20$, $t=2$ the finite hypothesis runs only to
$2p+2\ell+t=1038$, which the engine verifies. The last exception to
$G(n)=G(n-20)$ is at $n=517$, so $498$ is exactly the preperiod.
Flammenkamp's page states a sharper bound still --- $t+\max\{\texttt{last},
\texttt{depth}\}=2+\max\{200,37\}=202$ successive values suffice.
\texttt{verification/prove\_octal\_45\_periodicity.py} checks the hypothesis
window, both inclusions pair by pair over a band above the bound, the
preperiod, and the four engine statistics; full output in
\texttt{results/octal\_games/prove\_045\_periodicity\_output.txt}.

\section*{Entry 5: Audit --- the Fifth Miss, and What Caused It}
\textbf{Date:} September 25, 2026 \\
Flammenkamp's page carries two tables. The one I read gives per-game
sequences and periods. The one I did not read is headed \emph{Nontrivial
Octal-Games with known Structure} and ends in a \texttt{solved} column. Its
row for \texttt{.45} is
\begin{center}\footnotesize\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3.5pt}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrrrrrrrr}
\toprule
game & per. & prep. & miss & dens. & ty. & rare & l+t & mxG & dep. & au. & cor. & \textbf{solved} \\
\midrule
\texttt{.45} & 20 & 498 & 67 & 0.1345 & 0 & 11 & 200 & 8 & 37 & 1 & 0.4 & \textbf{1956} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
so the game was solved in 1956, and the four statistics I had reproduced as
engine validation (\texttt{miss}, \texttt{rare}, \texttt{last+t},
\texttt{maxG}) were sitting in the same row as the date that made the project
pointless. The page's $65$ unsettled $2$-place games are listed separately;
\texttt{.45} is not among them.

This is the fifth consecutive target that turned out to be known, but the
failure mode is new and worse. Misses 1--4 came from inferring openness from
absence of evidence --- nobody had written the result down where I looked. This
one came from reading a primary source that stated the answer explicitly, in a
column I did not parse, while extracting four other numbers from the same
row. Volume of verification did not help: every computational claim in
Entries 2 and 3 is correct, and two of them agree with the database to the
digit.

\textbf{Rule adopted.} Before any effort is committed to a target, every
column of every table in the primary source that names the target must be
identified by name, including columns not being used. A source consulted for
one field has not been read. Concretely: if a table has a \texttt{solved}
column, read it first; if the meaning of a column is unknown, that is a
blocking question, not a detail. A corollary applies to the literature gate
generally --- a positive, dated statement of openness must name the specific
game or parameter, not the family.

\textbf{What survives.} The engine (Entry 2), validated eight independent
ways. The re-derivation of the Guy--Smith shift argument (Entry 4), which is
correct mathematics and is now the thing I would cite. The negative record
itself, which is the reason this log exists.

\textbf{What does not.} Any claim of novelty for \texttt{0.45}; the bespoke
$S$/$C$/$R$ proof of Entry 4's superseded version; and the framing in the
previous abstract, which described the boundedness half as ``a complete,
gap-free proof'' of something already proved in 1956.
