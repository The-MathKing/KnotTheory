# Episode 4 — Why this is called a Riemann Hypothesis

Audience: knows what a Ramanujan graph is from episode three. No number theory assumed.

## E04S01 — The classical zeta function in two minutes

1. The question from last episode, which P of n, k are Ramanujan, has a second name in the project: the Riemann Hypothesis for P of n, k. This episode explains the name honestly. The short version: there is a function attached to every graph that imitates the most famous function in number theory, and the graph version of the famous unsolved conjecture turns out to be exactly the Ramanujan condition. The long version takes the rest of the episode.

2. Start with the original. The Riemann zeta function is the sum of one over n to the power s, for n running over all positive whole numbers: one plus one over two to the s, plus one over three to the s, and so on. For s equal to two, the sum is pi squared over six. For s equal to one it diverges. The function can be extended to complex values of s, and the Riemann Hypothesis is a statement about where it equals zero: all the zeros with real part between zero and one lie on the single vertical line where the real part of s is one half. It has been open since 1859.

3. Two features of zeta matter for us. First, Euler showed it is also a product over prime numbers: for each prime p, take one divided by one minus p to the minus s, and multiply them all together. That is called the Euler product, and it is why zeta knows about primes. Second, the zeros of zeta control how the primes are distributed: the prime number theorem, which says there are about x over log x primes below x, is equivalent to a statement about where zeta is nonzero. Zeros encode distribution.

## E04S02 — Prime cycles in a graph

4. Now graphs. What plays the role of a prime number in a graph? A closed walk: start at a vertex, follow edges, return to the start. With two restrictions. First, no backtracking: you may not step along an edge and immediately step back along it. Second, no repetition: the walk must not simply be a shorter closed walk traced twice or three times. A closed walk satisfying both, considered up to where you start on it, is called a prime cycle.

5. Example. In the triangle, three vertices all joined, going around zero, one, two, back to zero is a prime cycle of length three. Going around the other way, zero, two, one, zero, is a different prime cycle, because direction matters. Going around twice, length six, is not prime: it repeats the length-three one. Going zero to one and back to zero is not allowed: that is backtracking. So the triangle has exactly two prime cycles of length three, and more of longer lengths that wind around in more complicated ways once the graph is bigger.

6. In the four-cycle, going around once in each direction gives two prime cycles of length four, and nothing shorter, because the only way to return in fewer than four steps would be to backtrack. In the Petersen graph, the shortest cycles have length five, and there are twelve five-cycles, each traversable in two directions: twenty-four prime cycles of length five.

## E04S03 — The Ihara zeta function

7. In the nineteen sixties, Ihara defined a zeta function for graphs by copying Euler's product with prime cycles in place of primes. For each prime cycle C of length ell, take one divided by one minus u to the power ell, and multiply over all prime cycles. The variable u plays the role of p to the minus s: a prime of length ell contributes like a prime number of size q to the ell, where q is one less than the degree. For cubic graphs, q is two.

8. This product has an Euler product by definition, it satisfies a functional equation relating u to one over q u, and there is a prime number theorem for it, counting prime cycles of length ell by an exponential in ell. The analogy with number theory is exact enough that you can state the Riemann Hypothesis for it. Substitute u equals q to the minus s, and ask: do all the poles, the places where the function blows up, that lie in the strip zero less than real part of s less than one, sit on the line real part of s equals one half?

9. That is the graph Riemann Hypothesis. For the classical zeta it is about zeros; for the graph zeta the roles flip and it is about poles, because the graph function is defined as a product of reciprocals. The statement has the same shape: everything interesting sits on one vertical line.

## E04S04 — Ihara's determinant formula

10. Here is the difference from number theory, and it is the whole reason the graph question can be settled. Ihara proved that the graph zeta function is the reciprocal of a determinant. For a cubic graph with adjacency matrix A, the reciprocal of zeta of u equals one minus u squared, to the power number of edges minus number of vertices, times the determinant of I minus u A plus two u squared I, where I is the identity matrix.

11. We will not prove the determinant formula. But we can unwind what it says about poles. A pole of zeta is a zero of the reciprocal. The factor one minus u squared vanishes at u equals plus or minus one, which are outside the strip, so ignore them. The determinant vanishes when the matrix I minus u A plus two u squared I is singular, meaning it sends some nonzero vector to zero.

12. Now use eigenvectors. If x is an eigenvector of A with eigenvalue lambda, then this matrix times x is one minus u lambda plus two u squared, times x. So the determinant vanishes exactly when one minus lambda u plus two u squared equals zero for some eigenvalue lambda of A. A quadratic in u, one for each eigenvalue. The poles of zeta are the roots of these quadratics.

## E04S05 — The two-line computation

13. Solve the quadratic two u squared minus lambda u plus one equals zero. By the quadratic formula, u equals lambda plus or minus the square root of lambda squared minus eight, all over four. Now there are two cases, and this is the whole point.

14. Case one: lambda squared is less than eight, that is, the absolute value of lambda is less than two root two. Then lambda squared minus eight is negative, the square root is imaginary, and the two roots are complex conjugates. Their product is the constant term over the leading coefficient, one over two. Two conjugates have equal absolute value, so each has absolute value the square root of one half, which is one over root two.

15. Now translate back. We set u equal to two to the minus s. Absolute value of u equals one over root two means two to the minus real part of s equals two to the minus one half, so the real part of s is exactly one half. On the critical line. Case two: lambda squared is bigger than eight. Then the two roots are real, their product is still one half, but they are different, so one is bigger than one over root two and one is smaller. Real part of s is not one half for either. Off the line.

16. Check the numbers. For lambda equals one, the roots are zero point two five plus or minus zero point six six i, absolute value zero point seven zero seven, which is one over root two. For lambda equals minus two, the roots are minus zero point five plus or minus zero point five i, absolute value again zero point seven zero seven. For lambda equals two point nine, the roots are zero point eight eight five and zero point five six five, two different real numbers. Off the line.

17. So the poles coming from an eigenvalue lambda lie on the critical line exactly when the absolute value of lambda is at most two root two. The trivial eigenvalues three and minus three give the poles u equals one and one half, which lie at the edges of the strip and are excluded by convention. Put it all together: a cubic graph satisfies the Riemann Hypothesis if and only if every nontrivial eigenvalue has absolute value at most two root two. If and only if it is Ramanujan.

## E04S06 — What the name does and does not mean

18. So the graph Riemann Hypothesis is a theorem away from a finite eigenvalue check. That is what makes it decidable for a given graph, which the classical one is not for the integers. The project is explicit about this. The analogy lives in the zeta function, in the Euler product and the functional equation and the prime cycle theorem, not in the difficulty. Nobody is claiming to have done anything about the Riemann Hypothesis for the integers.

19. But it is a real definition, and it generates a real question with a real answer. For which n and k does P of n, k satisfy the Riemann Hypothesis? That is the project's first question, and it is exactly the question of which P of n, k are Ramanujan. From here on we will say Ramanujan, because that is what gets computed, and remember that it means the same thing.

20. One more piece of context. The eigenvalues of P of n, k were computed completely in 2011 by Gera and Stanica. Their paper gives a formula for every eigenvalue. But it never uses the words Ramanujan, expander, or spectral gap. The formula was there, the number two root two was there, and nobody had put them side by side. That is the gap the project fills. The difficulty, as we will see in episode eight, is that the formula contains nested square roots, and deciding whether a nested square root is below two root two when the two are almost equal is exactly where computers start guessing.

## E04S07 — Recap

21. What you should now be able to do. State the Riemann zeta function and its Euler product. Define a prime cycle in a graph and count them in a triangle. State the Ihara zeta function as a product over prime cycles. Explain, using eigenvectors, why its poles come from the quadratics two u squared minus lambda u plus one. Carry out the two-line computation showing that the roots have absolute value one over root two exactly when lambda squared is at most eight. And say in one sentence what the graph Riemann Hypothesis is and is not.
