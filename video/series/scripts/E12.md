# Episode 12 — Newton's method, interval arithmetic, and certified solutions

Audience: knows the goal, T equals the identity, from episodes ten and eleven. Newton's
method and interval arithmetic are built from root two.

## E12S01 — Solving equations numerically: Newton on root two

1. We now want a matrix on the P of n, k pattern, entries varying around the ring, whose monodromy is the identity. That is a system of polynomial equations in the entries. Polynomial systems are solved numerically, and the standard tool is Newton's method. Here it is on the simplest possible equation: x squared equals two.

2. Write f of x equals x squared minus two; we want f equals zero. Start with a guess, x equals one point five. f of one point five is zero point two five, not zero. The slope of f at one point five is two x, which is three. Newton's idea: follow the tangent line down to where it crosses zero. The tangent crosses at x minus f of x over slope: one point five minus zero point two five over three, which is one point four one six seven.

3. Repeat. f of one point four one six seven is zero point zero zero six nine; slope two point eight three three; new x is one point four one four two one six. Repeat again: one point four one four two one three five six two four, correct to twelve digits. Each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits. Three steps from a rough guess to twelve digits.

4. For a system of equations in many unknowns, the same thing works, with the slope replaced by the matrix of all partial derivatives, called the Jacobian, and the division replaced by solving a linear system. Start from a guess, compute the residual f, solve Jacobian times step equals minus f, update, repeat. For our monodromy problem the unknowns are the matrix entries around the ring, and the equations say T minus I is zero, entry by entry.

## E12S02 — What Newton gives you, and what it does not

5. Suppose Newton converges and reports entries for which T minus I is zero to fourteen decimal places. Is Z of P of n, k now proved to be two k plus two? No. The entries are floating-point numbers, and the equations hold only approximately. Maybe there is a true solution a hair away, and the floating-point answer is a slightly rounded version of it. Or maybe the equations have no exact solution at all nearby, and Newton found a point where the residual is small but not zero, the way x squared equals two has no solution among fractions with denominator one thousand even though one thousand four hundred fourteen over one thousand comes close.

6. We need a different kind of statement: inside this specific box of numbers, there exists an exact solution. That is a theorem about a region, not a measurement at a point. The tool for it is interval arithmetic.

## E12S03 — Interval arithmetic in one minute

7. An interval is a range of numbers, like one point four one to one point four two. Interval arithmetic computes with ranges. Adding two intervals: add the lower ends, add the upper ends. Multiplying: compute all four products of the endpoints and take the smallest and largest. Here is x squared for x in one point four one to one point four two: the products of endpoints are one point nine eight eight one, two point zero zero two two, and two point zero one six four, so x squared lies in one point nine eight eight one to two point zero one six four. Subtract two: f of x lies in minus zero point zero one one nine to zero point zero one six four.

8. The point is that the result is guaranteed. Whatever x actually is in that interval, x squared minus two really is in the computed range. Rounding is handled by always rounding lower ends down and upper ends up, so the guarantee survives floating point. An interval computation is a proof about every number in the range at once.

## E12S04 — Krawczyk's test, on root two

9. Now the test that turns a numerical solution into an existence proof, due to Krawczyk in 1969. Take a box X around the numerical solution, a centre point x zero inside it, and an approximate inverse slope Y, typically one over the slope at the centre. Define K of X as: x zero minus Y times f of x zero, plus the quantity one minus Y times f prime of X, times the interval X minus x zero. Here f prime of X is the slope evaluated over the whole box, as an interval. The theorem: if K of X lies strictly inside X, then X contains exactly one solution of f equals zero.

10. Why does it work? The map that sends x to x minus Y f of x has the solutions of f equals zero as fixed points, where it sends x to itself. K of X is an interval containing everything this map does to the box X. If K of X sits inside X, the map sends the box into itself, and a fixed point theorem, Brouwer's, says it must have a fixed point inside. And the factor one minus Y f prime being small means the map shrinks distances, which gives uniqueness.

11. Do it for root two. Box X equals one point four one to one point four two. Centre x zero equals one point four one five. f of x zero equals one point four one five squared minus two, which is zero point zero zero two two two five. Slope at the centre: two point eight three, so Y equals one over two point eight three, zero point three five three four. First piece: x zero minus Y f of x zero equals one point four one five minus zero point zero zero zero seven eight six, which is one point four one four two one four.

12. Second piece: f prime over the box is two X, the interval two point eight two to two point eight four. Y times that is zero point nine nine six five to one point zero zero three five. One minus that is the interval minus zero point zero zero three five to plus zero point zero zero three five. Multiply by X minus x zero, which is minus zero point zero zero five to plus zero point zero zero five: the result is minus zero point zero zero zero zero one eight to plus zero point zero zero zero zero one eight.

13. So K of X is one point four one four two one four plus or minus zero point zero zero zero zero one eight: the interval one point four one four one nine six to one point four one four two three one. That sits comfortably inside one point four one to one point four two. Conclusion: the interval one point four one to one point four two contains exactly one solution of x squared equals two. We have proved that root two exists and lies in that box, without ever computing it exactly. That is the shape of every certification in Part Two.

## E12S05 — Which equations to certify

14. For the monodromy problem there is a complication. T is a product of n matrices whose entries are fractions of the unknowns, so T minus I is a horrible function to put intervals through. And the equations are degenerate: at k equals three the map from entries to T has a fifty-one-dimensional image inside a sixty-four-dimensional space, which means thirteen equations are consequences of the others in a way that is hard to pin down. Krawczyk's test needs a square, nondegenerate system.

15. The project certifies the nullity directly instead. Nullity at least r means there is a two n by r matrix K of full rank with A K equals zero: r independent kernel vectors side by side. Fix the form of K: an identity block on r chosen rows, and unknown entries X elsewhere. Then the equation A of w times K of X equals zero, where w are the matrix entries, is bilinear: each term is one entry of w times one entry of X, or one entry of w alone. Degree two, whole-number coefficients. Its Jacobian is a matrix whose entries are linear in the unknowns, which makes the interval evaluation cheap and tight.

16. One subtlety nearly broke the project. The system A K equals zero has more equations than independent ones: because A is symmetric, K transpose A K is a symmetric r by r matrix, so exactly r choose two of the equations are consequences of the others. If you choose which equations to keep by numerical pivoting, you can silently keep the wrong ones and leave r choose two equations unproved, even though the test passes. For r equals eight that is twenty-eight unproved equations. The project had certified a result this way and the test had passed. The fix is structural: certify every equation on the non-pivot rows, plus only the upper triangle of the r by r block on the pivot rows, and the symmetry forces the lower triangle. A passing test is not a proof until you have checked that the test tests the right thing.

## E12S06 — Exact arithmetic, and the results

17. There is one more layer. Krawczyk's test needs three quantities bounded: the residual at the centre, the preconditioned Jacobian over the box, and a contraction constant alpha less than one. Computing those in floating point reintroduces rounding at the very last step. The project computes them exactly, as fractions. Every floating-point number is a fraction with a power-of-two denominator, so the box centre is an exact fraction. The system is bilinear with whole-number coefficients, so the residual and Jacobian at the centre are exact fractions. The preconditioner Y is arbitrary, so rounding it to a fraction costs nothing. The final comparison, alpha less than one, is a comparison of two whole numbers.

18. The only obstacle was speed: the exact computations involve products of matrices with about a thousand rows, with entries that are huge whole numbers. The solution was to write each whole number in base two to the twenty, with digits between minus two to the nineteen and two to the nineteen, so that every product of digits fits exactly into a floating-point number, and let fast matrix libraries do the digit arithmetic. Exact answers at floating-point speed.

19. With all of this in place, the certification goes through. Z of P of n, three equals M of P of n, three equals eight, for n equals seventeen through thirty-three, each one certified, including the primes seventeen, nineteen, twenty-three, twenty-nine, and thirty-one, which no rotation-invariant certificate could ever reach. But still one n at a time. The next episode turns one n at a time into every n at once.

## E12S07 — Recap

20. What you should now be able to do. Carry out three Newton steps for x squared equals two by hand. Explain why a numerical solution is not a proof. Do interval arithmetic: compute x squared minus two for x in one point four one to one point four two. State Krawczyk's test and explain the fixed-point idea behind it. Carry out the test for root two and verify that K of X lies inside X. Explain why the project certifies A K equals zero rather than T equals I. Explain the pivoting trap and its structural fix. And explain why the final test is done in exact fractions.
