# Episode 10 — Recurrences, and the exact ceiling on the matrix method

Audience: knows M at most Z from episode nine. Linear recurrences are taught from the
Fibonacci numbers before use.

## E10S01 — Recurrences: Fibonacci first

1. Before building any matrix, we will ask how far a matrix on the P of n, k pattern can possibly go. The answer uses an idea you may have seen with the Fibonacci numbers, so let us start there. Each Fibonacci number is the sum of the previous two: one, one, two, three, five, eight, thirteen. The rule, F of i plus one equals F of i plus F of i minus one, is called a linear recurrence of order two. Order two because each term is determined by the two before it.

2. Here is the key fact about a recurrence of order two. Once you know two consecutive terms, every later term is determined, and so is every earlier term, since you can run the rule backward: F of i minus one equals F of i plus one minus F of i. So a solution of the recurrence, a whole two-sided sequence, is completely pinned down by any two consecutive values. Change the starting pair from one, one to two, five, and you get a different sequence, two, five, seven, twelve, nineteen, obeying the same rule.

3. The set of all solutions therefore has two degrees of freedom: you choose two numbers, and the rest follows. Mathematicians say the solution space is two-dimensional. For a recurrence of order m, each term determined by the m before it, with the coefficient of the oldest term nonzero so that you can also run it backward, the solution space is m-dimensional. Choose m consecutive values, get everything.

4. One more notion. Suppose instead of an infinite line of positions, the positions go around a circle of length n, so position n is position zero again. A solution on the circle must be consistent with itself after going once around. Not every choice of m starting values survives the trip. Those that do are the solutions with period n, and they are the ones we care about.

## E10S02 — A matrix on P(n,k), and its kernel equations

5. Now take any matrix A with the P of n, k pattern. Not necessarily symmetric. Name its entries. On the outer edge from u i to u i plus one, entry b i; on the reverse direction, b i prime. On the spoke from u i to v i, entry c i, reverse c i prime. On the inner edge from v i to v i plus k, entry e i, reverse e i prime. On the diagonal, a i at u i and d i at v i. The pattern says every b, c, e, and their primes are nonzero, and the a's and d's are anything.

6. A kernel vector has an outer coordinate x i at each u i and an inner coordinate y i at each v i. A x equals zero gives one equation per vertex. The equation at outer vertex u i involves exactly the neighbours of u i: u i minus one, u i plus one, and v i, plus u i itself. Written out: b i minus one prime times x i minus one, plus a i x i, plus b i x i plus one, plus c i y i, equals zero.

7. That equation has exactly one inner coordinate in it, y i, and its coefficient c i is nonzero. So solve for it: y i equals minus one over c i, times b i minus one prime x i minus one plus a i x i plus b i x i plus one. Every inner coordinate is a three-term expression in the outer coordinates. The inner coordinates are not free. They are determined by the outer ones.

## E10S03 — Eliminating the inner coordinates

8. Now the equation at inner vertex v i. Its neighbours are v i minus k, v i plus k, and u i. The equation is e i minus k prime times y i minus k, plus d i y i, plus e i y i plus k, plus c i prime x i, equals zero. Three inner coordinates appear. Substitute the expression for each. y i minus k is a three-term expression in x centred at i minus k: positions i minus k minus one, i minus k, i minus k plus one. y i is centred at i. y i plus k is centred at i plus k.

9. The result is a single equation involving outer coordinates at nine positions: i minus k minus one, i minus k, i minus k plus one, then i minus one, i, i plus one, then i plus k minus one, i plus k, i plus k plus one. Nothing else. It is a linear relation among outer coordinates spread over a window from i minus k minus one to i plus k plus one, which has width two k plus three.

10. Look at the two extreme coefficients. The coefficient of x i plus k plus one comes only from the y i plus k term: it is minus e i times b i plus k over c i plus k. A product of nonzero edge entries divided by a nonzero spoke entry. Never zero. Likewise the coefficient of x i minus k minus one is minus e i minus k prime times b i minus k minus one prime over c i minus k. Never zero.

11. So this is a linear recurrence for the outer coordinates of order two k plus two: knowing two k plus two consecutive outer coordinates determines the next one, by solving for x i plus k plus one, and the previous one, by solving for x i minus k minus one. Exactly the Fibonacci situation, with two k plus two in place of two, and with coefficients that vary from position to position.

## E10S04 — Going once around: the monodromy

12. From the first section of this episode: a recurrence of order two k plus two on an infinite line has a solution space of dimension two k plus two. Choose two k plus two consecutive outer values; everything else follows. Now our positions are on a circle of length n. A kernel vector of A is a solution that is consistent after going once around: its values at positions n, n plus one, and so on must equal its values at zero, one, and so on.

13. Define the map T that takes a block of two k plus two consecutive values, runs the recurrence forward n steps, and reports the block two k plus two values further along, that is, the block that sits at the same place one full turn later. T is a linear map from two k plus two-dimensional space to itself: it is a two k plus two by two k plus two matrix. It is called the monodromy, from Greek for running around once. It is the product of n single-step matrices, one for each position on the circle.

14. A solution has period n exactly when going around once brings it back to itself: when T applied to its starting block gives the same block. In symbols, T v equals v, or T minus I times v equals zero. So the kernel vectors of A correspond exactly to the vectors v with T minus I times v equals zero. The nullity of A equals the nullity of T minus I. And since T minus I is a two k plus two by two k plus two matrix, its nullity is at most two k plus two, with equality exactly when T minus I is the zero matrix, that is, when T is the identity.

15. That is the ceiling theorem. For every matrix with the P of n, k pattern, symmetric or not, the nullity is at most two k plus two, and it equals two k plus two exactly when the monodromy is the identity. Every kernel vector corresponds to a starting block that returns to itself after one turn, and when T is the identity every block does.

## E10S05 — Why the ceiling matters

16. The number two k plus two was not new: it is the upper bound on Z. What is new is the identification. A matrix certificate for the lower bound can at best reach two k plus two, and the ceiling of what matrices can do coincides exactly with the bound they are trying to match. There is no slack on either side. Either a matrix certificate proves Z equals two k plus two, by having T equal the identity, or nothing in this family of methods can.

17. And there is a pleasing reason the number is the same. A nonzero kernel vector cannot vanish on two k plus two consecutive outer vertices, because the recurrence would then force it to vanish everywhere. But two k plus two consecutive outer vertices is exactly the forcing set from the 2020 upper bound. The order of the recurrence is the size of the forcing set. Two views of one fact.

18. An earlier version of the project described the matrix method as capped near six to eight, because the constructions tried so far stalled there. That was a property of those constructions, not of the method, and the theorem corrects it. The method is capped at two k plus two, and nothing less.

19. There is a second, very practical payoff. The ceiling is a bound that a computer search cannot legally exceed. Twice during the project, a numerical search returned a nullity larger than two k plus two. Both times, that was impossible by the theorem, so both times it was a bug in the search, and both times the bug was found. Wrapping a search inside a bound it cannot cross is the only thing that tells you an answer is wrong when it looks right.

## E10S06 — Recap

20. What you should now be able to do. Explain what a linear recurrence of order m is, using Fibonacci, and why its solution space is m-dimensional. Name the entries of a matrix with the P of n, k pattern. Write the kernel equation at an outer vertex and solve it for the inner coordinate. Explain why substituting into the inner equation gives a nine-term relation with nonzero extreme coefficients, hence a recurrence of order two k plus two. Define the monodromy T and explain why kernel vectors correspond to vectors fixed by T. State the ceiling theorem: nullity at most two k plus two, equality exactly when T is the identity. And explain why that means no slack between the matrix method and the forcing bound.
