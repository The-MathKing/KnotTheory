# Episode 1 — Graphs, and the Petersen family

Audience: someone with high-school algebra and no graph theory or linear algebra. Every
term is defined when first used, and every definition is followed by a concrete example.
Narration is read by a text-to-speech voice, so symbols are spelled out in words.

## E01S01 — What this series is

1. This is the first episode of a series that explains one mathematics research project from the ground up. The project is about a family of networks called generalized Petersen graphs, and it asks two questions about them. The questions are precise, and the answers are exact. But to even state the questions properly you need a handful of ideas: graphs, matrices, eigenvalues, complex numbers, polynomials. Each episode builds one of those ideas before using it. Nothing is assumed beyond algebra and a little trigonometry.

2. Here is the plan. Episodes one to three build the objects: graphs, then matrices and eigenvalues, then what eigenvalues tell you about a network. Episodes four to eight answer the first question, which is about the fixed network. Episodes nine to fourteen answer the second question, which is about every matrix that fits the network's shape. The last episode is about what is actually proved, what is open, and how a project like this checks its own numbers.

3. If something goes by too fast, that is what the pause button is for. Every worked example in this series uses real numbers that you can redo on paper, and you should. The point of the examples is that you can see the idea with your own hands before trusting the general statement.

## E01S02 — What a graph is

4. A graph, in this subject, is not a chart with axes. It is a collection of points, called vertices, and a collection of lines joining some pairs of them, called edges. That is the entire definition. The positions of the points on the page do not matter. The lengths of the lines do not matter. Only which pairs are joined.

5. Here is an example. Four vertices, which we will name zero, one, two, and three. Four edges: zero to one, one to two, two to three, and three back to zero. This graph is called the four-cycle, written C four, because the edges go around in a cycle. If I redraw it as a square, or as a bent diamond, or with the points scattered randomly, it is the same graph, because the same pairs are joined.

6. Two vertices joined by an edge are called neighbours, or adjacent. In the four-cycle, vertex zero has two neighbours, one and three. The number of neighbours a vertex has is called its degree. In C four every vertex has degree two. A graph in which every vertex has the same degree is called regular, and if that common degree is three it is called cubic. Cubic graphs are the stage for this entire project, so hold on to that word. Cubic means every vertex touches exactly three edges.

7. Here is a cubic graph: the cube itself, eight corners as vertices, twelve edges along its sides. Each corner meets three edges. And here is a graph that is not regular: a path of four vertices in a row. The two end vertices have degree one, the two middle vertices have degree two.

## E01S03 — The Petersen graph

8. Now the most famous small graph in mathematics. Draw five vertices in a ring, joined one to the next, around in a cycle. That is an outer five-cycle. Inside, draw five more vertices, but join each one not to its neighbour but to the vertex two steps around. Zero to two, two to four, four to one, one to three, three to zero. The result is a five-pointed star, a pentagram. Finally, join each outer vertex to the inner vertex sitting directly inside it. Those five connecting edges are called spokes.

9. Count. Ten vertices. Five outer edges, five inner edges, five spokes: fifteen edges. Each outer vertex touches two ring edges and one spoke: degree three. Each inner vertex touches two star edges and one spoke: degree three. So this is a cubic graph on ten vertices. It is called the Petersen graph, after Julius Petersen, who drew it in 1898. It shows up as a counterexample to almost every hopeful guess in graph theory, which is why it is famous.

10. The reason this project cares about it is a different feature, which we will come to in a moment: its symmetry. But first, let us generalize the drawing.

## E01S04 — Generalized Petersen graphs P(n,k)

11. In the Petersen graph there were five outer vertices, and the inner star stepped by two. Replace five by any number n, and two by any step k. That gives the generalized Petersen graph, written P of n, k. Two whole numbers go in, one graph comes out. The ordinary Petersen graph is P of five, two.

12. Here is the recipe precisely. Name the outer vertices u zero, u one, up to u n minus one, and place them in a ring. Join u i to u i plus one for each i, where the index wraps around: after n minus one comes zero again. Mathematicians say the indices are taken modulo n, which just means wrap around.

13. Name the inner vertices v zero to v n minus one. Join v i to v i plus k, again wrapping around. For k equal to one, this is just another ring. For k equal to two it is a star when n is five, and more generally it is a pattern that steps two at a time. For k equal to three it steps three at a time. Here are P of eight, one, P of eight, two, and P of eight, three, side by side. Same outer ring, different inner pattern.

14. Finally join u i to v i by a spoke. Every vertex now has degree three: two from its own ring or star, one from the spoke. So P of n, k is cubic, with two n vertices and three n edges, for every n and k.

15. One small rule: we only consider k smaller than n over two. The reason is that stepping by k and stepping by n minus k produce the same inner edges, just traced in the opposite direction, so P of n, k and P of n, n minus k are the same graph. Restricting to k below n over two avoids counting each graph twice. For example, with n equal to eight, k runs over one, two, and three only.

## E01S05 — Worked example: P(7,2) by hand

16. Let us build P of seven, two completely, on paper. Outer vertices u zero through u six in a ring. Outer edges: u zero to u one, u one to u two, and so on up to u six to u zero. Seven edges. Inner vertices v zero through v six. Inner edges, stepping by two: v zero to v two, v one to v three, v two to v four, v three to v five, v four to v six, v five to v zero, and v six to v one. That last one wraps around: six plus two is eight, and eight modulo seven is one. Seven edges.

17. Spokes: u zero to v zero, up through u six to v six. Seven edges. Total: twenty-one edges, fourteen vertices. Check the degree of v three: it is joined to v five, and to v one, since one plus two is three, and to u three by its spoke. Three neighbours. Cubic.

18. Notice how the inner edges of P of seven, two form a single seven-pointed star that visits every inner vertex: zero, two, four, six, one, three, five, back to zero. That happens because two and seven have no common factor. For P of eight, two the inner edges would instead form two separate four-cycles, the even vertices and the odd vertices, because two divides eight. Both are perfectly good generalized Petersen graphs. The inner pattern can be one loop or several.

## E01S06 — The symmetry

19. Now the feature the whole project runs on. Take P of n, k and add one to every index: u zero becomes u one, u one becomes u two, and so on around, and the same for the inner vertices. This is a rotation of the picture by one step. Does it preserve the graph? An outer edge u i to u i plus one goes to u i plus one to u i plus two, which is again an outer edge. An inner edge v i to v i plus k goes to v i plus one to v i plus one plus k, again an inner edge. A spoke u i to v i goes to u i plus one to v i plus one, again a spoke.

20. So the rotation sends edges to edges. It is a symmetry of the graph. Doing it n times brings everything back to where it started. In the language we will use later, the graph has a cyclic symmetry of order n. The fancy word is that the rotation is an automorphism, a relabelling of the vertices that preserves adjacency.

21. Why does this matter so much? Because a symmetry of a graph becomes a symmetry of every matrix built from the graph, and a matrix with a cyclic symmetry can be broken into much smaller pieces. For P of n, k, a matrix with two n rows and two n columns breaks into n separate two-by-two pieces. That one fact is used in almost every episode from episode five onward. For now, just remember: rotate by one step, the graph looks the same.

## E01S07 — Recap

22. What you should now be able to do. Define a graph: vertices and edges, positions irrelevant. Define degree, regular, and cubic. Draw the Petersen graph and explain why it is cubic. Draw P of n, k from the recipe: outer ring, inner step by k, spokes. Explain why k is kept below n over two. And explain why rotating every index by one is a symmetry.

23. Next episode: matrices. We will turn a graph into a table of numbers, learn what it means to multiply that table by a list of numbers, and meet the single most important idea in the project, the eigenvalue. We will do every computation on the four-cycle and the Petersen graph by hand.
