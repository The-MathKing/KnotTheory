# Episode 5 — Complex numbers, roots of unity, and how symmetry splits the matrix

Audience: knows eigenvalues from episode two. Complex numbers are built from scratch.

## E05S01 — Complex numbers as points in the plane

1. This episode builds the engine of the whole project. It needs complex numbers, so we start there, from nothing. A complex number is a point in the plane. The point with coordinates a and b is written a plus b i. The horizontal coordinate a is called the real part, the vertical coordinate b is the imaginary part. The number i itself is the point zero, one, straight up from the origin.

2. Adding complex numbers is adding coordinates: two plus i, plus one plus three i, is three plus four i. Multiplying is the interesting part. The rule is: multiply out as if i were a variable, and then replace i squared by minus one. For example, two plus i times one plus three i is two, plus six i, plus i, plus three i squared, which is two plus seven i minus three, which is minus one plus seven i.

3. Why minus one? Because of what multiplication does geometrically. Every complex number has a distance from the origin, its absolute value, and an angle measured from the positive horizontal axis. Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their distances and adds their angles. The number i has distance one and angle ninety degrees. So i times i has distance one and angle one hundred eighty degrees, which is the point minus one. That is i squared equals minus one, as geometry.

4. Here is the most useful consequence. A complex number with distance one and angle theta is cosine theta plus i sine theta. Multiplying by it rotates the plane by theta. We write it as e to the i theta, and you can treat that as a name for now: e to the i theta means the point on the unit circle at angle theta. The rule angles add becomes e to the i theta times e to the i phi equals e to the i theta plus phi, which is why the exponential notation is used.

## E05S02 — Roots of unity

5. Now a special set of points. Divide the unit circle into n equal arcs, starting at the point one on the positive axis. The n points you get are at angles zero, two pi over n, two times two pi over n, and so on. The first one after one, at angle two pi over n, is called zeta, or zeta n when we need to say which n. The others are its powers: zeta squared is at angle two times two pi over n, zeta cubed at three times, and zeta to the n is back at angle two pi, which is the point one.

6. So zeta to the n equals one. These n points are called the n-th roots of unity, because each one, raised to the n-th power, gives one. For n equal to four they are one, i, minus one, and minus i. For n equal to six they are the corners of a regular hexagon. For n equal to five they are the corners of a regular pentagon, at angles zero, seventy-two, one hundred forty-four, two hundred sixteen, and two hundred eighty-eight degrees.

7. One fact we will use over and over. Zeta to the minus j, which means one over zeta to the j, is the point at angle minus j times two pi over n, the mirror image of zeta to the j across the horizontal axis. Add them: zeta to the j plus zeta to the minus j. The imaginary parts cancel, the real parts add, and you get two cosine of two pi j over n. A real number. This is the formula that converts roots of unity into cosines, and cosines are what the rest of the project computes with.

8. Check it for n equal to four, j equal to one: zeta is i, zeta inverse is minus i, the sum is zero, and two cosine of ninety degrees is zero. For n equal to six, j equal to one: zeta is at sixty degrees, the sum is two cosine of sixty, which is one. Correct.

## E05S03 — The cycle: eigenvectors from roots of unity

9. Now the trick, first on the simplest graph with a rotation symmetry: the n-cycle, vertices zero to n minus one in a ring. Its adjacency matrix sends a vector x to the vector whose entry at i is x i minus one plus x i plus one, the two neighbours, indices wrapping around.

10. Take the vector whose entry at vertex i is zeta to the i j, for some fixed j. Entries one, zeta to the j, zeta to the two j, and so on. What does A do to it? At vertex i, it adds the neighbours: zeta to the i minus one times j, plus zeta to the i plus one times j. Factor out zeta to the i j: you get zeta to the i j, times the quantity zeta to the minus j plus zeta to the j. And that quantity is the number from a moment ago: two cosine of two pi j over n.

11. So A times this vector is two cosine of two pi j over n, times the same vector. It is an eigenvector, with eigenvalue two cosine of two pi j over n. One for each j from zero to n minus one. That is all n eigenvalues of the cycle, found by one line of algebra. For n equal to four they are two cosine of zero, ninety, one eighty, two seventy degrees: two, zero, minus two, zero. The same list we found by guessing in episode two. For n equal to ten they are two, one point six one eight, zero point six one eight, minus zero point six one eight, minus one point six one eight, minus two, and then the same values again as j runs past five.

12. Why did it work? Because the vector zeta to the i j has the property that shifting every index by one just multiplies it by zeta to the j. Shifting is the symmetry of the cycle. Vectors that the symmetry merely scales are automatically well-behaved under every matrix that respects the symmetry. That is the general principle, and it is called the discrete Fourier transform, though we will never need that name.

## E05S04 — Two rings and the two-by-two blocks

13. Now P of n, k. It has two rings of vertices, outer and inner, and the rotation shifts both at once. So try a vector with two unknown amplitudes: put p times zeta to the i j on outer vertex u i, and q times zeta to the i j on inner vertex v i. The numbers p and q are to be found; j is fixed.

14. Apply the adjacency matrix. At outer vertex u i, sum the three neighbours: u i minus one and u i plus one carry p zeta to the i minus one, j and p zeta to the i plus one, j, whose sum is p zeta to the i j times two cosine two pi j over n. And v i carries q zeta to the i j. So the result at u i is zeta to the i j, times the quantity alpha j p plus q, where alpha j stands for two cosine two pi j over n.

15. At inner vertex v i, the neighbours are v i minus k and v i plus k, carrying q zeta to the i minus k, j and q zeta to the i plus k, j, whose sum is q zeta to the i j times two cosine of two pi j k over n. Call that number beta j. And u i carries p zeta to the i j. So the result at v i is zeta to the i j times p plus beta j q.

16. So the big matrix A acting on our two-amplitude vector does something very simple to the pair p, q: it sends it to alpha j p plus q, and p plus beta j q. That is a two-by-two matrix, with alpha j and beta j on the diagonal and ones off the diagonal, acting on the pair p, q. Call it M j. If p, q is an eigenvector of M j with eigenvalue lambda, then our big vector is an eigenvector of A with the same eigenvalue lambda.

17. Each M j has two eigenvalues, and there are n values of j, giving two n eigenvalues in total, which is all of them. The two n by two n matrix A has been split into n two-by-two matrices. Alpha j comes from the outer ring stepping by one. Beta j comes from the inner ring stepping by k. The off-diagonal ones come from the spokes, which connect outer to inner without any rotation.

## E05S05 — Worked example: the Petersen spectrum from five blocks

18. Let us recover the Petersen spectrum, n equals five, k equals two. We need alpha j equals two cosine of seventy-two j degrees and beta j equals two cosine of one hundred forty-four j degrees, for j from zero to four.

19. j equals zero: alpha is two cosine zero, which is two, and beta is two. The block has rows two, one and one, two. We solved that matrix in episode two: eigenvalues three and one. There is the trivial eigenvalue three, from j equals zero, which always gives the all-ones vector.

20. j equals one: alpha is two cosine seventy-two degrees, which is zero point six one eight. Beta is two cosine one hundred forty-four degrees, which is minus one point six one eight. Trace: zero point six one eight minus one point six one eight equals minus one. Determinant: zero point six one eight times minus one point six one eight, minus one, equals minus one minus one, equals minus two. The eigenvalues solve lambda squared plus lambda minus two equals zero, which factors as lambda minus one times lambda plus two. Eigenvalues one and minus two.

21. Those two cosines are golden ratio numbers: two cosine seventy-two is the golden ratio minus one, and two cosine one forty-four is minus the golden ratio, and their product is exactly minus one. That is why the determinant came out to a whole number.

22. j equals two: alpha is two cosine one forty-four, minus one point six one eight, and beta is two cosine two eighty-eight, which equals two cosine seventy-two, zero point six one eight. The same two numbers, swapped. Same trace, same determinant, same eigenvalues: one and minus two. j equals three and j equals four are mirror images of j equals two and j equals one, since cosine of minus theta is cosine theta. Each gives one and minus two again.

23. Total: three and one from j equals zero; one and minus two from each of j equals one, two, three, four. Three once, one five times, minus two four times. Exactly the Petersen spectrum from episode two, now derived from five two-by-two matrices, each solved by trace and determinant.

## E05S06 — Two curves, sampled

24. Now step back and look at the structure. For a two-by-two matrix with alpha and beta on the diagonal and ones off it, the eigenvalues are alpha plus beta over two, plus or minus the square root of alpha minus beta over two, squared, plus one. That is the quadratic formula applied to lambda squared minus trace lambda plus determinant.

25. Fix k and let the angle vary continuously: t instead of two pi j over n. Alpha becomes two cosine t, beta becomes two cosine k t, and the two eigenvalues become two curves in t, an upper branch and a lower branch. These curves depend on k but not on n. The actual eigenvalues of P of n, k are the two curves sampled at the n equally spaced angles two pi j over n.

26. Here is the picture for k equal to three. Two curves between minus three and three. The dashed lines are at plus and minus two root two. The dots are the samples for a given n. The graph is Ramanujan exactly when no sample, other than the trivial ones, lands outside the dashed lines. As n grows, the samples get denser. And if a curve pokes outside the dashed lines anywhere, a dense enough sample will eventually land in that region. Watch what happens as n goes from ten to forty: the dots near t equals zero creep up the curve until one crosses the line.

27. That single observation is the whole of the first question. The curves are fixed by k. The sampling gets finer with n. The forbidden regions are where the curves exceed two root two in absolute value. So for each k, only finitely many n can avoid them. The next episode makes this exact: it turns the curve condition into the sign of a polynomial.

## E05S07 — Recap

28. What you should now be able to do. Add and multiply complex numbers, and explain multiplication as rotation. Define the n-th roots of unity and show that zeta to the j plus zeta to the minus j is two cosine two pi j over n. Show that the vector zeta to the i j is an eigenvector of the cycle, with eigenvalue two cosine two pi j over n. Derive the two-by-two block M j for P of n, k with alpha j and beta j on the diagonal. Recompute the Petersen spectrum from the five blocks. And explain the picture of two fixed curves sampled at n points.
