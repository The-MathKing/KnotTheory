# Episode 11 — Rotation-invariant certificates: roots on the grid, and where they run out

Audience: knows the two-by-two block trick from episode five and the ceiling from episode
ten. Galois conjugates are introduced with root two before use.

## E11S01 — The simplest matrices: the same weights everywhere

1. We want matrices on the P of n, k pattern with nullity two k plus two. Start with the simplest kind: matrices that look the same after the rotation. Every outer edge gets the same entry, call it one after scaling. Every spoke gets the same entry c. Every inner edge gets the same entry e. Every outer diagonal entry is a, every inner diagonal entry is d. Four numbers, a, c, d, e, and the matrix is symmetric. We call such a matrix rotation-invariant, or period one, because its entries repeat with period one around the ring.

2. A rotation-invariant matrix commutes with the shift, so the trick of episode five applies word for word. Put p zeta to the i m on the outer vertices and q zeta to the i m on the inner ones. The outer equation gives a plus s m times p plus c q, where s m is two cosine two pi m over n, from the two outer neighbours each weighted one. The inner equation gives c p plus d plus e t m times q, where t m is two cosine two pi k m over n, from the two inner neighbours each weighted e. So the matrix splits into n two-by-two blocks M m, with a plus s m and d plus e t m on the diagonal and c off the diagonal.

3. The nullity of the whole matrix is the number of blocks that are singular, that is, that have determinant zero. A singular two-by-two block with a nonzero off-diagonal entry c has exactly a one-dimensional kernel, so contributes exactly one to the nullity. So: nullity equals the number of m from zero to n minus one with a plus s m times d plus e t m minus c squared equal to zero.

## E11S02 — The symbol: a polynomial in s

4. The determinant depends on m through s m and t m. But t m is a polynomial in s m. Two cosine of two theta equals two cosine theta squared minus two, so for k equals two, t equals s squared minus two. For k equals three, t equals s cubed minus three s. In general t equals a Chebyshev-type polynomial in s of degree k. Check k equals two with s equals two cosine sixty, which is one: t should be two cosine one twenty, minus one, and s squared minus two is one minus two, minus one. Correct.

5. So the determinant is a polynomial in s of degree k plus one: a plus s, times d plus e times a degree k polynomial in s, minus c squared. Call it the symbol, F of s. The nullity of the matrix is the number of m for which F of s m is zero. That is: the number of grid points s m that are roots of the symbol. The grid is the set of numbers two cosine two pi m over n for m from zero to n minus one.

6. Notice the grid's shape. Two cosine two pi m over n and two cosine two pi n minus m over n are equal, since cosine is even. So every grid value except two, at m equals zero, and minus two, at m equals n over two when n is even, is hit exactly twice. The grid has roughly n over two distinct values, each counted twice. A root of the symbol at a doubly-hit grid value contributes two to the nullity.

## E11S03 — Worked example: nullity six on P(12,2)

7. Take n equals twelve, k equals two. The grid values two cosine of thirty degrees times m are: two, root three, one, zero, minus one, minus root three, minus two, and then back up. So the distinct values are root three, one, zero, minus one, minus root three, each hit twice, and two and minus two once each. The symbol has degree three. If its three roots are three of the doubly-hit values, the nullity is six, which is two k plus two. The ceiling.

8. Choose roots zero, minus one, and minus root three. Then the symbol must be e times s times s plus one times s plus root three, with some leading coefficient e. Expand: e times s cubed plus one plus root three s squared plus root three s. Now match that against the general form. The general symbol for k equals two is a plus s times d plus e s squared minus two, minus c squared. Expand that: e s cubed plus a e s squared plus d minus two e s plus a d minus two e minus c squared.

9. Match coefficients. s cubed: e equals e, fine. s squared: a e equals e times one plus root three, so a equals one plus root three, about two point seven three two. s: d minus two e equals e root three, so d equals two plus root three times e. Constant: a times d minus two e minus c squared must be zero, so c squared equals a times root three e equals root three times one plus root three times e, which is three plus root three times e. Take e equals one: a is two point seven three two, d is three point seven three two, c squared is four point seven three two, c is two point one seven five.

10. So here is an explicit symmetric matrix on P of twelve, two: diagonal two point seven three two on the outer ring, three point seven three two on the inner ring, ones on the outer edges, one on the inner edges, and two point one seven five on the spokes. Its symbol vanishes at s equals zero, minus one, and minus root three, each hit by two values of m. Nullity six. Computing the eigenvalues numerically confirms it: exactly six are zero. Since Z of P of twelve, two is at most six by the forcing bound, we have proved Z equals M equals six for P of twelve, two, with one matrix.

11. Why did c squared come out positive? It did not have to. Watch what happens with a different choice of roots.

## E11S04 — The antipodal obstruction

12. Choose roots one, zero, and minus one instead. Symbol: e s times s minus one times s plus one, which is e s cubed minus e s. Match: s squared coefficient a e equals zero, so a equals zero. s coefficient d minus two e equals minus e, so d equals e. Constant: a d minus two e minus c squared equals zero, and a is zero, so c squared equals zero. c equals zero. But c is the spoke entry and must be nonzero. This choice of roots is not realisable by any matrix with the pattern. The symbol exists as a polynomial but no matrix produces it.

13. The pattern is general. For k equals two, the constant term works out to c squared equals minus s one plus s two times s one plus s three times s two plus s three, where s one, s two, s three are the three roots. If two roots are negatives of each other, antipodal on the grid, a factor vanishes and c squared is zero. So: you may prescribe three roots, but no two of them may be antipodal, and the product must have the right sign for c to be real. In the first example, roots zero, minus one, minus root three: the three pairwise sums are minus one, minus root three, and minus one minus root three. Their product is minus three minus root three. Negate it: c squared is three plus root three. Positive, and it matches what we found by matching coefficients.

14. This settles k equals two for every n by counting. For n at least nine, other than n equals ten, the grid has three doubly-hit values with no antipodal pair, so Z of P of n, two equals M equals six. At n equals eight the grid values are plus or minus root two and zero, every triple contains an antipodal pair, and the true value of Z is in fact five: the method is right to fail. At n equals ten the grid values are plus or minus one point six one eight and plus or minus zero point six one eight, again every triple has an antipodal pair, but the true value of Z is six: here the method falls short. For n equals ten a different matrix, not rotation-invariant, with whole-number entries, does the job, found later in the project.

## E11S05 — Galois conjugates: roots that come in bundles

15. For k larger than two, the symbol has degree k plus one but still only three adjustable quantities: a, d, e, and c squared, which are four numbers but with one used up by the leading coefficient. So you can prescribe three roots and no more. Three roots give nullity six, well short of two k plus two when k is three or more. Unless the other roots land on the grid by themselves. Here is how that can happen.

16. Root two is a root of x squared minus two. So is minus root two. If a polynomial with rational coefficients has root two as a root, it automatically has minus root two as a root as well, because dividing by x squared minus two leaves no remainder. The two numbers are called conjugates: any polynomial with rational coefficients that vanishes at one vanishes at the other. They are algebraically indistinguishable.

17. Now the grid numbers. Two cosine of seventy-two degrees, zero point six one eight, is a root of x squared plus x minus one. The other root is minus one point six one eight, which is two cosine of one hundred forty-four degrees. Conjugates. Two cosine of two pi over seven, one point two four seven, has minimal polynomial x cubed plus x squared minus two x minus one, whose other roots are two cosine of four pi over seven and two cosine of six pi over seven. Three conjugates, all on the grid for n equal to seven or any multiple of seven. In general the conjugates of two cosine two pi over d are exactly the numbers two cosine two pi m over d with m coprime to d. The set of them is called a Galois orbit.

18. So: build the symbol as a product of such minimal polynomials, with rational coefficients. Prescribe one root two cosine two pi over d, and the whole orbit comes for free, every member on the grid whenever d divides n. That is how the project gets whole-number certificates with nullity ten at k equals four, valid whenever sixty, seventy, or ninety divides n, and nullity twelve at k equals five whenever twenty-four divides n. These were the first exact values of Z at k equals four and five at which the bound two k plus two is attained, and the first valid for infinitely many n.

## E11S06 — Where the method stops, exactly

19. These certificates come with a limitation that is intrinsic, not a failure of effort. A symbol with fixed rational coefficients has fixed roots, specific algebraic numbers. A fixed algebraic number lies on the grid for n only when n is in one divisibility class: the two cosine two pi over d root is on the grid exactly when d divides n. A divisibility class contains at most one prime number. So no rotation-invariant matrix can ever give a value of Z valid for all large n. It can do multiples of ten at k equals three, multiples of sixty at k equals four. It can never do n equals seventeen, nineteen, or twenty-three.

20. Krishnan's conjecture asks for all n at least thirteen. The route to all n must break the rotational symmetry: use matrices whose entries vary around the ring. For those, there are no two-by-two blocks and no symbol. But there is still the monodromy from episode ten. And the condition T equals the identity has no roots of unity in it, no divisibility, nothing arithmetic. It is a system of polynomial equations in the entries, and it can be solved for any n, prime or not. The next episode is about solving it, and about proving that a solution found by a computer is real.

## E11S07 — Recap

21. What you should now be able to do. Write the two-by-two block of a rotation-invariant matrix on P of n, k. Explain why the nullity is the number of grid values that are roots of the symbol. Reproduce the n equals twelve, k equals two example: choose roots zero, minus one, minus root three, match coefficients, get a, d, and c squared. Show that roots one, zero, minus one force c equals zero, and explain the antipodal obstruction. Explain what conjugates and Galois orbits are using root two and two cosine seventy-two. And explain why a fixed symbol can only ever cover one divisibility class of n.
