# Episode 8 — Deciding exactly: why floating point is not enough, and the complete classification

Audience: knows the test "Q at least zero on the grid" and the bound n at most 230 from
episode seven. Floating-point numbers and algebraic numbers are explained from scratch.

## E08S01 — The finite computation

1. Episode seven ended with a finite problem. For every n from three to two hundred thirty and every k from one to below n over two, decide whether D k of cosine two pi j over n is at least zero at every nontrivial j. That is thirteen thousand one hundred ten pairs, each needing up to a hundred or so evaluations of a polynomial. A computer does this in seconds. So why is there a whole episode about it?

2. Because a finite computation is not yet a proof. Remember P of twenty-three, two and P of twenty-four, two: the values of D two were plus zero point two one seven and minus zero point three five two. Comfortable margins. But as k grows, the polynomial D k has degree two k plus two, with coefficients in the millions, and at a band edge its value at a grid point can be a number like zero point zero zero zero zero zero one, or minus that. Whether it is slightly positive or slightly negative is the whole question. Can a computer tell?

## E08S02 — How computers store numbers, and where they lie

3. A computer stores a real number in floating point: a sign, about fifty-three binary digits, and an exponent. That is about sixteen decimal digits of precision. Any number that needs more digits is rounded. One tenth, for example, is a repeating fraction in binary, so it is stored as the nearest fifty-three-bit fraction, which is zero point one plus about five times ten to the minus eighteen. Then zero point one plus zero point two in floating point is not zero point three but zero point three zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero zero four. Harmless usually. Not harmless when the sign of a difference of two nearly equal numbers is the answer.

4. Evaluating D k of u j involves computing a cosine, raising it to powers up to two k plus two, multiplying by large coefficients, and adding terms that nearly cancel. Each step rounds. The accumulated error can easily be ten to the minus ten or worse for large k. If the true value is ten to the minus twelve, the computed sign is a coin flip. A classification that depends on coin flips is not a classification.

5. So the project needed a way to decide the sign of D k of u j that cannot be wrong. The way is to notice what kind of number D k of u j actually is.

## E08S03 — Algebraic integers, in one example

6. Root two is not a fraction, but it is the root of a polynomial with whole-number coefficients and leading coefficient one: x squared minus two. Numbers like that are called algebraic integers. The golden ratio is one: it is a root of x squared minus x minus one. Two cosine of seventy-two degrees is one: it is a root of x squared plus x minus one. Two cosine of two pi over seven is one: a root of x cubed plus x squared minus two x minus one. Every number two cosine two pi j over n is an algebraic integer, because it equals zeta to the j plus zeta to the minus j, and zeta is a root of x to the n minus one.

7. Sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers. D k of u j is built from two cosine values by adding, multiplying, and multiplying by whole numbers. So D k of u j is an algebraic integer. More precisely, it lives in the number system you get by taking whole numbers and the root of unity zeta and combining them with plus and times. Mathematicians write that system as Z adjoined zeta.

8. And here is the key property. In that number system there is an exact way to represent every element: as a list of whole numbers. Zeta satisfies a specific polynomial, the n-th cyclotomic polynomial, and every element of Z adjoined zeta can be written uniquely as a combination of one, zeta, zeta squared, up to zeta to the degree of that polynomial minus one, with whole-number coefficients. Working with these lists of whole numbers, every addition and multiplication is exact. No rounding, ever. In particular, you can decide exactly whether an element is zero: it is zero exactly when every whole number in its list is zero.

## E08S04 — Deciding the sign exactly

9. So here is the method. Compute D k of u j as an exact element of Z adjoined zeta, a list of whole numbers. First test whether it is zero. If every coefficient is zero, the value is exactly zero, the eigenvalue is exactly two root two, and the graph passes, since the condition is at most. If it is not zero, we need its sign, and for that we need to know how far from zero a nonzero element can be.

10. There is a theorem for that: a separation bound. A nonzero algebraic integer of this kind, with a known degree and known coefficient sizes, has absolute value at least a specific tiny number. For these values the bound is two hundred forty-nine to the power minus degree minus one. It is small, but it is a positive number that we can compute. So: compute the floating-point value, and compute an upper bound on the floating-point error. If the floating-point value clears the separation bound by more than the error, its sign is certain. If it does not, refuse to answer, and recompute with more precision until it does.

11. That is the discipline. Floating point is allowed to propose a sign. It is never allowed to decide one on its own. Every one of the thirteen thousand one hundred ten cases was decided this way, and a second, independent program computed every case in plain floating point as a control. Where exact and control disagree, something is wrong, and you go look.

## E08S05 — The bug the control caught

12. The control earned its keep. At k equals one, the inner ring has the same step as the outer ring, so the inner and outer terms in the exact computation involve the same power of zeta. The code built those terms as a lookup table from exponents to coefficients, using a notation that silently keeps only one entry when two have the same key. So at k equals one, half the terms disappeared. The exact classifier declared every P of n, one Ramanujan, including P of nine, one, whose spectrum actually contains minus two point eight seven nine, which is below minus two root two.

13. The floating-point control flagged four disagreements, all at k equals one. The bug was found in an afternoon and fixed. Without the control, the error would have reached the paper, in the one place where exactness was the whole point. Two independent methods running side by side is not redundancy. It is the only thing that tells you an answer is wrong when it looks right.

## E08S06 — The classification

14. Here is the answer. Exactly four hundred sixty pairs n, k, with n at least three and k below n over two, give a Ramanujan graph. Since P of n, k is the same graph as P of n, l whenever l is plus or minus k or plus or minus the inverse of k modulo n, those four hundred sixty pairs describe three hundred twenty-four distinct graphs. The largest n that occurs is one hundred twelve, at k equals forty-one. The largest k is forty-five. Nothing with k above forty-five is Ramanujan.

15. Plot the survivors as dots, n horizontal, k vertical. The Dirichlet line n equals two hundred thirty-one is far off to the right; the data stops at one hundred twelve. The bound only had to be uniform, not sharp. For small k the survivors form unbroken runs. k equals two: every n from five to twenty-three. k equals four: every n from nine to forty-two. Those endpoints are the B k ceilings from episode six.

16. For odd k the runs thin out to even n only in their upper half. That is the parity law from episode seven. Look at k equals nine: every n from nineteen to forty-six, then only even n up to ninety. The odd n were cut off at half the constant, by the band at u equals minus one.

17. And at the top of the picture, past k equals twenty-five, no even k survives at all. Only odd k, up to forty-five, because only odd k gets the bipartite exemption for minus three. The last members alive are kept alive by that exemption alone.

18. The picture is not monotone in k. k equals nine reaches n equals ninety, but k equals eight stops at forty-nine and k equals ten at fifty-two. For even k, some interior band binds before the band at u equals one does. The project records the result as a certified list, not a formula: nothing proves that the survivors stop at k equals forty-five except having checked every case up to the Dirichlet line.

## E08S07 — What Part One was, honestly

19. Step back. An analytic question about where a zeta function has poles. Ihara's theorem turned it into an eigenvalue bound. The rotation symmetry turned the eigenvalue bound into the sign of a polynomial on a grid. Two squarings, each justified, cleared the radicals. A change of variable removed k from the geometry. Dirichlet's theorem made the family finite in both parameters. Exact arithmetic in Z adjoined zeta certified the finitely many survivors.

20. The project rates its own contribution carefully: exactness and completeness, not surprise. Similar classifications had been done for other families of graphs, by Droll for unitary Cayley graphs, and by Le and Sander for integral circulant graphs, with the same shape of answer: finitely many. Anyone who knew that rotating a fixed base cannot give an infinite expander family would have predicted the shape. What could not have been predicted is which four hundred sixty, and that nothing survives past k equals forty-five.

21. That is the first question answered. The second question starts next episode, and it uses the same graph, the same symmetry, and the same grid of roots of unity. But the matrix is no longer fixed. Instead of asking about the eigenvalues of the one adjacency matrix, we will let every nonzero entry be any real number, and ask how many times the eigenvalue zero can be made to appear.

## E08S08 — Recap

22. What you should now be able to do. Explain what a floating-point number is and why zero point one plus zero point two is not exactly zero point three. Explain why the sign of a tiny value cannot be trusted from floating point alone. Define an algebraic integer and give three examples. Explain why D k of u j is an exact list of whole numbers in Z adjoined zeta, and how that decides zero exactly. Explain the separation bound and the accept-or-refuse rule. Tell the story of the k equals one bug. And state the classification: four hundred sixty pairs, three hundred twenty-four graphs, n at most one hundred twelve, k at most forty-five.
