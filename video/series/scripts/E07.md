# Episode 7 — The corner criterion, and why the family is finite

Audience: knows D_k from episode six. Product-to-sum identities, AM-GM, and Dirichlet's
theorem are each explained with numbers before use.

## E07S01 — D_k is a difference of squares

1. D k was seven plus P squared minus eight A squared. Eight A squared is two root two A squared. So D k is a difference of two squares, and it factors: D k equals f minus times f plus, where f minus is seven plus P minus two root two A and f plus is seven plus P plus two root two A. Those two factors are exactly the two conditions from last episode: f minus at least zero is the upper eigenvalue condition, f plus at least zero is the lower one.

2. A small but useful fact: the two factors cannot both be negative. Their sum is two times seven plus P, which is at least six because P is at least minus four. Two numbers with a positive sum cannot both be negative. So D k is negative exactly when one factor is negative, and the test D k at least zero is the same as the test: the smaller of f minus and f plus is at least zero. Which is: seven plus P minus two root two times absolute value of A is at least zero. That is the form we had, now seen as a factor.

## E07S02 — Two cosine identities

3. Now a change of variable that will make k vanish. We need two identities from trigonometry, each checkable in a minute. First, product to sum: cosine of a times cosine of b equals one half of cosine of a plus b plus cosine of a minus b. Second, sum to product: cosine of a plus cosine of b equals two cosine of a plus b over two, times cosine of a minus b over two.

4. Check the first with a equals b equals sixty degrees: cosine sixty squared is one quarter, and one half of cosine one twenty plus cosine zero is one half of minus one half plus one, which is one quarter. Check the second with a equals sixty, b equals zero: cosine sixty plus cosine zero is one and a half, and two cosine thirty times cosine thirty is two times three quarters, which is one and a half. Both hold.

5. Apply them with a equals t and b equals k t. Define x as cosine of k plus one t over two, and y as cosine of k minus one t over two. Then the sum-to-product identity says cosine t plus cosine k t equals two x y, so A, which is two cosine t plus two cosine k t, equals four x y. And the product-to-sum identity says cosine t cosine k t equals one half of cosine k plus one t plus cosine k minus one t. Using the double angle formula on each, cosine k plus one t is two x squared minus one and cosine k minus one t is two y squared minus one. So cosine t cosine k t is x squared plus y squared minus one, and P, which is four cosine t cosine k t, equals four x squared plus four y squared minus four.

## E07S03 — The function Q: k has left the geometry

6. Substitute into the smaller factor: seven plus P minus two root two absolute A becomes seven plus four x squared plus four y squared minus four, minus two root two times four absolute x y. Simplify: four x squared plus four y squared minus eight root two absolute x y plus three. Call this Q of x, y. The graph is Ramanujan if and only if Q is at least zero at every nontrivial grid point, where the grid point j gives x equals cosine of pi j k plus one over n and y equals cosine of pi j k minus one over n.

7. Now look at Q. It has no k in it. For every k, the test is the same function of the same two variables. The parameter k has moved out of the function and into the map that places the points: it decides which x, y pairs get tested, but not what test is applied to them. That is a genuine simplification. One fixed region in the x, y plane, the set where Q is negative, and the only question is whether the grid, mapped in, lands a point there.

8. And the region is tiny. Q equals zero is a hyperbola, and inside the square from minus one to one in both x and y, it cuts off four congruent pieces, one at each corner. Each piece meets the sides of the square in segments of length three halves minus root two, about zero point zero eight six. Their total area is zero point four three percent of the square. For every k, P of n, k is Ramanujan if and only if its mapped grid avoids four small corners.

## E07S04 — Worked example: Q on P(5,2) and P(24,2)

9. Redo the two examples from last episode with Q. P of five, two, j equals one: x is cosine of pi times three over five, cosine one hundred eight degrees, minus zero point three zero nine. y is cosine of pi times one over five, cosine thirty-six degrees, zero point eight zero nine. Q is four times zero point zero nine five five plus four times zero point six five four five minus eight root two times zero point two five zero plus three. That is zero point three eight two plus two point six one eight minus two point eight two eight plus three, which is three point one seven two. Positive, and nowhere near the corner.

10. P of twenty-four, two, j equals one: x is cosine of three pi over twenty-four, cosine of twenty-two point five degrees, zero point nine two three nine. y is cosine of pi over twenty-four, cosine seven point five degrees, zero point nine nine one four. Both close to one: the point is near the corner one, one. Q is four times zero point eight five three six plus four times zero point nine eight three zero minus eight root two times zero point nine one six zero plus three. That is three point four one four plus three point nine three two minus ten point three six three plus three, which is minus zero point zero one seven. Negative, just barely. P of twenty-four, two fails because its j equals one point lands just inside the corner.

11. Compare with P of twenty-three, two: x is zero point nine one seven two, y is zero point nine nine zero seven, and Q comes out to plus zero point zero one zero five. Just outside the corner. The difference between passing and failing is a few hundredths, which is exactly why episode eight will insist on exact arithmetic.

## E07S05 — A bound for every k: the AM–GM argument

12. Last episode gave a ceiling B k on n for each k, but B k grows with k. We want one bound on n that works for every k at once. Here is the argument, and it uses nothing beyond three elementary facts, each of which you will see with numbers.

13. Set up. Let x be cosine t and y be cosine k t, where t is the grid angle for j equals one, that is, two pi over n. Note these are a different x and y from the corner section; here they are the plain cosines. Let a be one minus x, b be one minus y, and s be a plus b. Both a and b are between zero and two. The failure condition, two root two A greater than seven plus P, with A equals two x plus two y and P equals four x y, rearranges to G greater than seven where G is four root two times x plus y minus four x y. Substitute x equals one minus a and y equals one minus b and expand: G equals eight root two minus four, minus four root two minus four times s, minus four a b. You can check the constant: at a equals b equals zero, G is eight root two minus four, and directly, four root two times two minus four is eight root two minus four.

14. Fact one, the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means: for nonnegative a and b, four a b is at most a plus b squared. Why: a plus b squared minus four a b equals a minus b squared, which is never negative. With numbers: a equals one, b equals three: four times three is twelve, four squared is sixteen. So minus four a b is at least minus s squared, and G is at least eight root two minus four minus four root two minus four times s minus s squared. Failure happens as soon as that lower bound exceeds seven, that is, as soon as s squared plus four root two minus four times s is less than eight root two minus eleven.

15. Fact two: the left side, s squared plus four root two minus four times s, is increasing in s for s nonnegative, and at s equals tau, where tau is three minus two root two, about zero point one seven one six, it equals exactly eight root two minus eleven. Check: tau squared is seventeen minus twelve root two, and four root two minus four times tau is twenty root two minus twenty-eight. Add them: seventeen minus twenty-eight is minus eleven, and minus twelve plus twenty is eight root two. Eight root two minus eleven. So s less than tau forces failure.

16. Fact three: one minus cosine theta is at most theta squared over two, for every theta. This is the statement that the cosine curve lies above the parabola one minus theta squared over two, which you can see on a plot, and prove by calculus in two lines. With numbers: theta equals one radian, one minus cosine one is zero point four six, and one half is zero point five. So a is at most t squared over two and b is at most k t squared over two, and s is at most one plus k squared times t squared over two.

17. Put the pieces together. Failure is forced when s is less than tau, which is guaranteed when one plus k squared times t squared over two is less than tau, that is, when t is less than the square root of two tau over k squared plus one. Square root of two tau is two minus root two. So failure when t is less than two minus root two over the square root of k squared plus one. The smallest grid angle is t equals two pi over n. So a Ramanujan graph needs two pi over n at least that, which rearranges to n at most pi times two plus root two times the square root of k squared plus one, after multiplying top and bottom by two plus root two.

18. For k equals two this is pi times three point four one four times root five, which is twenty-three point nine eight. The true ceiling was twenty-three point three seven, so the uniform bound is slightly weaker, as it must be, since it holds for all k at once. For k equals three it gives thirty-three point nine against the true thirty-two point five.

19. An honest note recorded in the project. An earlier draft derived the same constant by expanding the eigenvalue in a Taylor series in t at fixed k. That derivation was invalid: the critical t is of size one over k, so k t is of size one, outside the range where the expansion is accurate. The constant was right by luck; the argument was wrong. The proof you just saw replaces it, and uses no expansion at all.

## E07S06 — Dirichlet's theorem closes the family

20. We still have a problem. For each k, n is bounded, but the bound grows like k. Could there be Ramanujan graphs with enormous k and enormous n? To rule that out we need a different tool. Return to the failure condition in the form: some nontrivial j has one minus cosine two pi j over n plus one minus cosine two pi j k over n less than tau. The trick is that j need not be one. Any j works, and for huge n there are many to choose from.

21. Write it with distances to the nearest whole number. For any real theta, write double bars theta for the distance from theta to the nearest integer; for example, double bars of two point nine equals zero point one, and double bars of zero point four equals zero point four. Since cosine of two pi theta only depends on theta modulo one, one minus cosine two pi theta is at most two pi squared times double bars theta squared, by fact three. So the failure condition is implied by: double bars of j over n squared plus double bars of j k over n squared is less than tau over two pi squared, which is about zero point zero zero eight six nine.

22. So we need a j that makes both j over n and j k over n nearly whole numbers at once. j over n is small when j is small. j k over n is the hard one: we need j k to be nearly a multiple of n. That is a problem about approximating a fraction, k over n, by fractions with small denominators j. And there is a classical theorem that does exactly this.

23. Dirichlet's approximation theorem. For any real number r and any whole number J, there is a whole number j between one and J such that j times r is within one over J plus one of a whole number. The proof is the pigeonhole principle. Take the J plus one numbers zero times r, one times r, up to J times r, and keep only their fractional parts, between zero and one. Cut the interval from zero to one into J plus one equal bins of width one over J plus one. If two of the fractional parts land in the same bin, subtract: the difference of the two multiples is some j times r, with j between one and J, within one bin width of a whole number. If no two share a bin, then every bin holds exactly one, so the last bin, just below one, holds the fractional part of some m times r with m at least one, and that m times r is within one bin width of the next whole number. Either way the theorem holds.

24. With numbers: r equals zero point four one four and J equals four. The fractional parts of zero r, one r, two r, three r, four r are zero, zero point four one four, zero point eight two eight, zero point two four two, and zero point six five six. Five bins of width zero point two. They land one per bin, so we are in the second case: the last bin, from zero point eight to one, holds zero point eight two eight, which is two r. So j equals two: two r is zero point eight two eight, within zero point one seven two of the whole number one, and zero point one seven two is less than the promised one fifth.

25. Apply it with r equals k over n and J equal to the whole-number part of root n. We get a j at most root n with double bars of j k over n at most one over J plus one, which is less than one over root n. And since j is at most root n, j over n is at most one over root n, so double bars of j over n is also less than one over root n. Square both and add: the sum is less than two over n.

26. So failure is forced whenever two over n is less than tau over two pi squared, that is, whenever n exceeds four pi squared over tau, which is two hundred thirty point zero nine seven. For every n of at least two hundred thirty-one, and every k, P of n, k is not Ramanujan. Combined with k less than n over two, the entire family of candidates lives in a finite triangle: n from three to two hundred thirty, k from one to n over two. About thirteen thousand pairs. The question has become a finite computation.

## E07S07 — The parity law, and no infinite family from any base

27. Two consequences before the computation. First, the parity law promised last episode. When n is even and k is odd, the index j equals n over two maps to x equals cosine of pi k plus one over two, and y equals cosine of pi k minus one over two. Since k plus one and k minus one are even, both cosines are plus or minus one: the point is one of the four corners of the square, inside a forbidden region. But that index carries the eigenvalue minus three of a bipartite graph, and minus three is exempt. For odd n there is no such index, and the nearest grid point instead lands just inside the band at u equals minus one. So for odd k, odd n is cut off at half the constant that even n is. Being bipartite is worth a factor of two in n. For even k there is no band at minus one and no such law.

28. Second, a general observation. Nothing in the argument used P of n, k beyond two facts: the graph is built from a small base by rotating, and it is cubic. In episode fourteen we will see this construction in general; it is called a cyclic cover. For any fixed base graph, the block at j equals one converges, as n grows, to the base's own adjacency matrix, whose top eigenvalue is three. So some nontrivial eigenvalue of the cover is dragged up toward three, past two root two. No infinite family of Ramanujan graphs can be built by rotating a fixed base. This was known in spirit, and it is why the famous Ramanujan families use non-commutative groups rather than rotations. What the project adds is the explicit constant and the exact criteria.

## E07S08 — Recap

29. What you should now be able to do. Factor D k as f minus times f plus and explain why both factors cannot be negative. State and check the two cosine identities. Derive A equals four x y and P equals four x squared plus four y squared minus four. Write Q of x, y and explain why it has no k. Evaluate Q on P of five, two and P of twenty-four, two. Reproduce the AM GM argument and its three facts to get n at most pi two plus root two root k squared plus one. State Dirichlet's theorem and prove it by pigeonhole. And derive the bound n at most two hundred thirty.
