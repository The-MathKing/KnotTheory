# Episode 9 — Zero forcing: a colouring game, and why it bounds the nullity

Audience: knows kernel and nullity from episode two. The game, the matrix connection,
and the published state of knowledge are all built from scratch.

## E09S01 — The second question

1. Part Two begins. Take the Petersen graph and its adjacency matrix: ones where there are edges, zeros elsewhere. Now forget the actual numbers and keep only the pattern: which positions are nonzero. Off the diagonal, a position is nonzero exactly when there is an edge. On the diagonal, we allow anything, including zero. Every symmetric matrix that fits this pattern is a matrix of the graph. The adjacency matrix is one of them. The matrix with twos on the edges and minus seven on the diagonal is another. There are infinitely many.

2. For each such matrix, recall from episode two the nullity: the number of independent vectors x with A x equals zero, equivalently the number of times zero appears as an eigenvalue. The second question is: over every symmetric matrix with the pattern of the graph G, how large can the nullity be? That largest value is called the maximum nullity of G, written M of G.

3. Why ask? Because it is a question about what the shape of a network forces on every system living on it. A physical system, a molecule, a circuit, a quantum spin chain, is often described by a symmetric matrix whose nonzero pattern is a graph; the exact values depend on details, but the pattern is fixed by the connections. Maximum nullity asks how degenerate the eigenvalue zero can be, given only the connections. Computing M directly means searching an infinite family of matrices. The tool that tames it is a colouring game.

## E09S02 — The game

4. Zero forcing is played on a graph with two colours. Start by colouring some set S of vertices blue; every other vertex is white. Then apply one rule, as many times as you can: if a blue vertex has exactly one white neighbour, that neighbour turns blue. We say the blue vertex forces it.

5. If, when the rule can no longer be applied, every vertex is blue, then S is called a zero forcing set. The zero forcing number of G, written Z of G, is the smallest possible size of a zero forcing set. Smallest number of initial blues that eventually colour everything.

6. Watch it on a path of six vertices in a row. Colour the left endpoint blue. It has exactly one neighbour, which is white, so it forces that neighbour blue. That vertex has two neighbours, one blue and one white: exactly one white, so it forces the next. And so on down the line. One initial blue colours the whole path. Z of the path is one.

7. Now a cycle of eight vertices. Colour one vertex blue. It has two neighbours, both white. Two white neighbours, not one: the rule does not apply. Stuck. Colour two adjacent vertices blue instead. Each now has exactly one white neighbour, so each forces outward, and the two fronts sweep around the cycle in opposite directions until they meet. Two initial blues suffice, and one does not, so Z of the cycle is two.

8. The Petersen graph. Try the five outer vertices. Each outer vertex has neighbours: two outer, both blue, and one inner, white. Exactly one white neighbour, so each forces its inner vertex. Everything is blue. So Z of Petersen is at most five. Can four do it? It turns out no, by checking every four-vertex set, so Z of the Petersen graph is five.

## E09S03 — The kernel, with a tiny example

9. Now connect the game to matrices. First a reminder of what A x equals zero means, on the smallest interesting example: the path on three vertices, zero, one, two, with the matrix having zero on the diagonal and ones on the two edges. Multiplying by this matrix sums each vertex's neighbours. So A x equals zero says: at vertex zero, the sum of its neighbours is zero, which means x one equals zero. At vertex one, x zero plus x two equals zero. At vertex two, x one equals zero.

10. So the kernel consists of vectors with x one equals zero and x two equals minus x zero: all multiples of one, zero, minus one. One independent direction. Nullity one. Now change the matrix: put a seven on the diagonal at vertex one. The equation at vertex one becomes x zero plus seven x one plus x two equals zero, and the others are unchanged: x one equals zero from both ends. Then x zero plus x two equals zero again, and the kernel is still multiples of one, zero, minus one. Nullity one. Now put a three on the diagonal at vertex zero: the equation at vertex zero becomes three x zero plus x one equals zero. With x one equals zero from vertex two's equation, x zero must be zero, then x two must be zero. Nullity zero. The diagonal matters.

## E09S04 — Why forcing bounds the nullity

11. Here is the argument that turns the game into a matrix fact, and it fits in one slide. Take any matrix A with the pattern of G: off the diagonal, nonzero exactly on edges; diagonal free. Suppose x is in the kernel, A x equals zero, and suppose we know x is zero on some set B of vertices. Colour B blue. Blue means: the kernel vector is known to vanish there.

12. Now suppose some blue vertex u has exactly one white neighbour, call it w. Look at the equation at row u: A u u times x u, plus the sum over neighbours v of u of A u v times x v, equals zero. Vertex u is blue, so x u is zero, killing the first term. Every neighbour of u other than w is blue, so those x v are zero, killing their terms. What remains is A u w times x w equals zero. And A u w is nonzero, because u and w are joined by an edge and the pattern puts a nonzero there. So x w must be zero. The kernel vector vanishes at w too. Colour w blue.

13. That is exactly the colour change rule. A blue vertex with one white neighbour forces that neighbour to vanish. So if S is a zero forcing set, and a kernel vector vanishes on S, then by playing the game it vanishes everywhere: it is the zero vector. Which means a kernel vector is completely determined by its values on S. There can be at most as many independent kernel vectors as there are vertices in S. The nullity of A is at most the size of S.

14. Take S to be a smallest zero forcing set: the nullity of A is at most Z of G. And this held for every matrix with the pattern, symmetric or not, so the maximum nullity M of G is at most Z of G. That inequality, M at most Z, is the bridge between the game and the matrices. It was discovered in 2008 by a group of mathematicians at a workshop on exactly this problem, and the game got its name from it: the blue set forces zeros.

## E09S05 — Why the inequality is useful

15. Look at what the inequality buys. To show Z of G is at most some number, exhibit one forcing set of that size and play the game. Easy. To show Z of G is at least some number, you must show that every smaller set fails. For a graph with fifty vertices and a candidate size of eight, that means checking hundreds of millions of sets. Brutal, and it gets worse exponentially.

16. But M is at most Z. So if you can find a single matrix with the pattern whose nullity is eight, then M is at least eight, so Z is at least eight. One matrix replaces hundreds of millions of set checks. A matrix is a certificate: an object you can hand someone, who can verify the nullity by elimination in a minute, without redoing any search. That is the strategy of Part Two: lower bounds on Z by building matrices with large nullity. And when the matrix nullity matches a forcing set size, both M and Z are known exactly, and they are equal.

## E09S06 — What was known about P(n,k)

17. For the generalized Petersen graphs, here is the state of knowledge before the project. In 2020, Rashidi, Shajareh Poursalavati and Tavakkoli proved that Z of P of n, k is at most two k plus two. The forcing set is two k plus two consecutive outer vertices, and the forcing sweeps around the graph like the cycle example, with the inner vertices getting forced along the way. The argument works because of the rotation symmetry.

18. They also proved Z of P of n, two equals six for all n at least ten, and claimed, as their Theorem 3.6, that Z of P of n, three equals eight for all n at least twelve.

19. That claim is false. In July 2026, Krishnan exhibited a zero forcing set of size seven in P of twelve, three. The published proof had a case analysis that never excluded seven-vertex sets. Krishnan computed the exact values of Z of P of n, three for n from seven to twenty: six, six, six at n equals seven, eight, nine; then eight at n equals ten; then seven at n equals eleven and twelve; then eight at every n from thirteen to twenty. He stated as Conjecture 5 that Z of P of n, three equals eight for every n at least thirteen, and wrote that what was missing was a lower-bound proof valid for all large n.

20. And for k at least four, essentially nothing was known: a single value, Z of P of two k plus one, k equals six for each k, far below the upper bound two k plus two. The open problem was explicit: find the threshold at k equals three, and give any rigorous lower bound for k at least four.

## E09S07 — What a lower bound for all n requires

21. Rigorous lower bound for all large n. That phrase is the whole of the rest of this series. Upper bounds come free from a forcing set. The lower bound needs, for every n, a matrix on the P of n, k pattern with nullity two k plus two. Not one matrix for n equals seventeen found by a search, and another for eighteen found by another search, and so on forever. A construction that works uniformly in n, with a proof that it works for all of them.

22. Episode ten asks how far any matrix on this pattern can go and finds an exact ceiling. Episode eleven builds the first certificates and finds why they cannot reach every n. Episodes twelve and thirteen break the symmetry and reach every n.

## E09S08 — Recap

23. What you should now be able to do. Define a matrix with the pattern of a graph, and the maximum nullity M. State the colour change rule and play it on a path, a cycle, and the Petersen graph. Define Z. Compute the kernel of a three-vertex path by hand and see why the diagonal matters. Reproduce the one-slide argument that a kernel vector vanishing on a forcing set is zero, and conclude M at most Z. Explain why a matrix is a certificate for a lower bound on Z. And state what was known for P of n, k: the upper bound two k plus two, the k equals two result, the false theorem, and Krishnan's Conjecture 5.
