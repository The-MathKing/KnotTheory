# Episode 3 — What eigenvalues tell you: random walks, the spectral gap, and Ramanujan graphs

Audience: knows what an adjacency matrix and an eigenvalue are from episode two. Nothing
else assumed.

## E03S01 — A random walk on a graph

1. Here is a game that turns eigenvalues into something you can watch. Put a token on vertex u zero of the Petersen graph. Each second, the token moves to one of its three neighbours, chosen at random with equal probability, one third each. Where is the token after one second? After ten?

2. Keep track not of the token but of the probabilities. Write p for the vector whose entry at vertex i is the probability that the token is at vertex i. At the start, p is one at u zero and zero everywhere else. After one step, the token is at each of u zero's three neighbours with probability one third. So p becomes one third at u one, one third at u four, one third at v zero, and zero elsewhere.

3. After the next step, each of those three vertices sends its one third onward, split three ways. A vertex's new probability is the sum, over its neighbours, of the neighbour's old probability divided by three. Sum over neighbours, divided by three. That is exactly the adjacency matrix from last episode, divided by three. The new p is one third times A times the old p. So the probability vector after t steps is one third A, applied t times, to the starting vector.

## E03S02 — Watching the walk forget

4. Here are the actual numbers for the Petersen graph, computed by applying one third A repeatedly. After one step, the probability is one third on three vertices and zero on seven. After three steps, every vertex has probability between zero and about zero point two. After five steps, every entry is within zero point zero five of one tenth. After seven steps, within zero point zero two three. The walk is converging to the uniform distribution, one tenth at every vertex, and it is converging fast.

5. Why one tenth? Because the all-ones vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue three, so one third A times the uniform vector is the uniform vector itself. Uniform is a fixed point. The question is how fast you get there, and the answer is in the other eigenvalues.

6. Here is the idea, and it is the single most useful idea about eigenvectors. Any vector can be written as a combination of eigenvectors, when the matrix is symmetric. So write the starting vector p as a sum: some multiple of the all-ones eigenvector, plus some multiple of an eigenvector with eigenvalue one, plus some multiple of an eigenvector with eigenvalue minus two, and so on. Now apply one third A. Each eigenvector piece just gets multiplied by its eigenvalue over three. The all-ones piece gets multiplied by three over three, which is one. The eigenvalue-one pieces get multiplied by one third. The eigenvalue-minus-two pieces get multiplied by minus two thirds.

7. Apply it t times. The all-ones piece stays. The eigenvalue-one pieces shrink like one third to the power t. The eigenvalue-minus-two pieces shrink like two thirds to the power t, flipping sign each step. After seven steps, two thirds to the seventh is about zero point zero five nine. The deviation we measured was zero point zero two three, which is that factor times the size of the piece. The slowest-shrinking piece controls the convergence, and it belongs to the eigenvalue with the largest absolute value other than three. For the Petersen graph that is minus two.

## E03S03 — The spectral gap

8. So here is the general principle. On a cubic graph, a random walk forgets its starting point at a rate set by the second-largest eigenvalue in absolute value. Call it lambda two. The pieces that are not uniform shrink like lambda two over three, to the power t. If lambda two is close to three, that ratio is close to one, and the walk barely moves toward uniform: the graph has bottlenecks, regions the token gets stuck in. If lambda two is well below three, the walk mixes quickly: the graph is well connected everywhere.

9. The difference three minus lambda two is called the spectral gap. Big gap, fast mixing, no bottlenecks. Small gap, slow mixing. A graph with a large spectral gap is called an expander, because from any set of vertices, the edges leaving the set are plentiful: the set expands.

10. Compare two graphs with ten vertices. The ten-cycle, a ring, has eigenvalues two cosine of two pi j over ten, which we will derive in episode five. The largest is two and the next is two cosine of thirty-six degrees, about one point six two. The ratio is zero point eight one, so a walk on the ring needs many steps to spread around. The Petersen graph has ratio two thirds, zero point six seven, and spreads in a handful of steps. Of course the ring has degree two and the Petersen graph has degree three, so this is not entirely fair, but it shows what the number means.

## E03S04 — How small can the second eigenvalue be?

11. Now the natural question. If a small lambda two means a well-connected graph, how small can we make it? Could there be cubic graphs, with more and more vertices, whose second eigenvalue stays tiny? The answer is no, and there is a hard floor.

12. The theorem is due to Alon and Boppana, from the nineteen eighties. For cubic graphs, as the number of vertices goes to infinity, the second eigenvalue cannot stay below two times the square root of two. More precisely: for any number epsilon greater than zero, every sufficiently large cubic graph has a nontrivial eigenvalue bigger than two root two minus epsilon. Two root two is about two point eight two eight.

13. Where does that strange number come from? Imagine unrolling a cubic graph. Start at a vertex, walk to its three neighbours, from each of those to its two further neighbours, and so on, never going back, and pretend you never return to a vertex you have seen. You get an infinite tree in which every vertex has exactly three neighbours. Every cubic graph is a folded-up version of this tree. The tree's own spectrum, in a suitable sense, is the whole interval from minus two root two to two root two.

14. Here is where the number comes from, in one paragraph. Count closed walks of length two t on the tree that start and end at the same vertex. In a tree there are no cycles, so a walk can only return by retracing: every step away must be paired with a later step back. The number of ways to arrange t steps out and t steps back so that you never go below the start is a Catalan number, which grows roughly like four to the t. And each step away has two fresh choices of direction, giving two to the t. The product grows like eight to the t over two t steps, which is the square root of eight, that is two root two, per step. The growth rate of closed walks is the largest eigenvalue in the infinite limit, so the tree's top eigenvalue is two root two. A finite cubic graph looks locally like the tree, and over long walks the tree's number leaks through. That is the content of the Alon Boppana theorem; the full proof makes this leakage precise.

## E03S05 — Ramanujan graphs

15. So two root two is the best possible value of lambda two for large cubic graphs. A cubic graph that already achieves it, whose nontrivial eigenvalues all have absolute value at most two root two, is as well connected as any large cubic graph can ever be. Such graphs were named Ramanujan graphs by Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak in 1988, because the first constructions used a deep result of number theory connected to Ramanujan's work.

16. The word nontrivial needs explaining. The eigenvalue three is always there and does not count: it is the trivial eigenvalue. There is a second exception. If the graph is bipartite, meaning its vertices split into two sides with every edge going from one side to the other, then minus three is also an eigenvalue: put plus one on one side and minus one on the other, and every vertex sums three neighbours of the opposite sign. That minus three is also trivial and also does not count. Everything else is nontrivial and must sit between minus two root two and two root two.

17. Here is the definition in one line. A cubic graph is Ramanujan if every eigenvalue other than three, and other than minus three when the graph is bipartite, has absolute value at most two root two.

18. Check the Petersen graph. Eigenvalues three, one, and minus two. Three is trivial. One and minus two are both inside plus or minus two point eight two eight. So the Petersen graph is Ramanujan. In fact with spectral gap two, it is an exceptionally good expander for its size.

## E03S06 — The first question of the project

19. Now the first question can be stated exactly. The generalized Petersen graphs P of n, k are cubic. For each pair n and k, we can compute all two n eigenvalues, throw away the trivial ones, and ask whether the rest have absolute value at most two root two. Which P of n, k are Ramanujan?

20. A first guess might be: lots of them, maybe all of them. We will see in episodes six and seven that the truth is the opposite. For each k, only finitely many n work, and in fact there are only finitely many pairs n, k in total: exactly four hundred sixty of them, and none at all with k bigger than forty-five. The road to that answer goes through complex numbers, a polynomial, and a theorem about approximating fractions. Each step is one episode.

21. Before that, one episode of context: why mathematicians call this question the Riemann Hypothesis for the graph. The name sounds grand. The next episode explains exactly what it means and exactly what it does not.

## E03S07 — Recap

22. What you should now be able to do. Explain a random walk on a graph as repeated multiplication by one third A. Explain why the walk converges to uniform, and why the speed is set by the second-largest eigenvalue in absolute value. Define the spectral gap and the word expander. State the Alon Boppana floor, two root two, and say roughly where it comes from. State what a Ramanujan graph is, including the two trivial eigenvalues. And verify that the Petersen graph is Ramanujan from its spectrum.
