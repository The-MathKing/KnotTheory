# Episode 6 — From an eigenvalue test to a polynomial: the function D_k

Audience: knows the two-by-two blocks from episode five. Polynomials, cosine identities, and
careful squaring are all explained.

## E06S01 — The condition, written out

1. Last episode left us here. P of n, k is Ramanujan when, for every j other than the trivial ones, both eigenvalues of the block M j lie between minus two root two and two root two. The block has alpha on one diagonal entry, beta on the other, and ones off the diagonal, where alpha is two cosine two pi j over n and beta is two cosine two pi j k over n. Its eigenvalues are alpha plus beta over two, plus or minus the square root of alpha minus beta over two, squared, plus one.

2. We want to decide this condition exactly, with no rounding. The trouble is the square roots. The eigenvalue has a square root in it, and the number two root two is a square root. Deciding whether one square root is less than another, when they are nearly equal, is where a computer with decimal numbers starts guessing. This episode gets rid of every square root, carefully, and ends up with a polynomial with whole-number coefficients.

3. First, some shorthand. Write capital A for alpha plus beta, and capital P for alpha times beta. Then the eigenvalues are A over two plus or minus half the square root of alpha minus beta squared plus four. And note two facts for later: alpha and beta are each between minus two and two, since they are two times a cosine. So A is between minus four and four, and P is between minus four and four.

## E06S02 — A warning about squaring

4. To remove a square root you square both sides of an inequality. But squaring can lie. Minus three is less than two, and yet nine is not less than four. Squaring preserves an inequality only when both sides are nonnegative. So every time we square, we have to check that both sides are nonnegative, and if they are, the squared inequality is equivalent to the original, not merely implied by it. Equivalent means we can go back. That is what makes the final polynomial an exact test and not just a necessary condition.

5. We will square twice. Each time, the check will be a one-line bound using the fact that A and P are between minus four and four. Keep an eye out for those two checks; they are the entire rigour of the episode.

## E06S03 — First squaring

6. Start with the larger eigenvalue. The condition that it is at most two root two reads: A over two plus half the square root of alpha minus beta squared plus four, is at most two root two. Multiply by two and move A across: the square root of alpha minus beta squared plus four is at most four root two minus A.

7. Check before squaring. The left side is a square root, so nonnegative. The right side is four root two minus A. Four root two is about five point six six, and A is at most four. So the right side is at least one point six six, positive. Both sides nonnegative: squaring is an equivalence.

8. Square: alpha minus beta squared plus four is at most four root two minus A squared, which expands to thirty-two minus eight root two A plus A squared. Now a small identity: A squared minus alpha minus beta squared equals alpha plus beta squared minus alpha minus beta squared equals four alpha beta equals four P. So replace alpha minus beta squared by A squared minus four P. The inequality becomes A squared minus four P plus four is at most thirty-two minus eight root two A plus A squared. The A squared cancels. Rearrange: eight root two A minus four P is at most twenty-eight. Divide by four: two root two A minus P is at most seven.

9. That is the condition for the upper eigenvalue. The lower eigenvalue being at least minus two root two is the mirror image: replace A by minus A, which gives minus two root two A minus P is at most seven. Both together say: two root two times the absolute value of A is at most seven plus P.

## E06S04 — Second squaring

10. One square root is left, the two root two. To remove it, square again. Check first: the left side, two root two times absolute value of A, is nonnegative. The right side is seven plus P, and P is at least minus four, so seven plus P is at least three. Positive. Squaring is again an equivalence.

11. Square: eight A squared is at most seven plus P squared. Rearranged: seven plus P squared minus eight A squared is at least zero. No square roots anywhere. Define the function D as seven plus P squared minus eight A squared. The block is Ramanujan-safe exactly when D is at least zero.

12. Pause on what happened. We started with two inequalities involving nested square roots and ended with one inequality saying a certain expression is nonnegative. The two checks, right side at least one point six six and right side at least three, are what guarantee that nothing was lost or gained on the way.

## E06S05 — Cosines become a polynomial: Chebyshev

13. Now turn D into a polynomial. A and P are built from alpha equals two cosine t and beta equals two cosine k t, where t is the angle two pi j over n. Write u for cosine t. Then alpha is two u. What about cosine k t? Here is the key fact: cosine of k t is a polynomial in cosine of t, with whole-number coefficients.

14. For k equals two it is the double angle formula: cosine two t equals two cosine squared t minus one, which is two u squared minus one. For k equals three: cosine three t equals four cosine cubed t minus three cosine t, which is four u cubed minus three u. In general the polynomial is called the k-th Chebyshev polynomial, written T k of u, and it satisfies T k plus one equals two u T k minus T k minus one, which lets you build them all from T zero equals one and T one equals u. Check: two u times u minus one is two u squared minus one. Correct.

15. So beta is two T k of u, A is two u plus two T k of u, and P is four u T k of u. Substitute into D: D k of u equals seven plus four u T k of u, squared, minus eight times two u plus two T k of u, squared. Every piece is a polynomial in u with whole-number coefficients, so D k is a polynomial in u with whole-number coefficients. Its degree is two k plus two, from squaring the degree k plus one term u times T k.

16. Here it is for k equals two, fully expanded. T two is two u squared minus one. Then seven plus four u T two is seven plus eight u cubed minus four u, and two u plus two T two is four u squared plus two u minus two. Square each, subtract eight times the second from the first, and collect: D two of u equals sixty-four u to the sixth, minus one hundred ninety-two u to the fourth, minus sixteen u cubed, plus one hundred twelve u squared, plus eight u, plus seventeen. Six coefficients, all integers. You can verify the expansion on paper in about ten minutes, and you should at least check the constant term: seven squared minus eight times two squared is forty-nine minus thirty-two, seventeen.

## E06S06 — Worked example: D_2 on three graphs

17. The test now reads: P of n, k is Ramanujan if and only if D k of cosine two pi j over n is at least zero for every nontrivial j. Let us use D two on P of five, two, the Petersen graph. The only nontrivial grid point is j equals one, since j equals two, three, four give the same cosine values up to sign and order. u equals cosine seventy-two degrees, which is zero point three zero nine. Plug into D two: the result is twenty-eight, exactly. Positive. Petersen is Ramanujan, as we knew.

18. Now P of twenty-three, two. The j equals one point is u equals cosine of two pi over twenty-three, which is zero point nine six two nine. D two there is zero point two one seven. Positive, but small. And P of twenty-four, two: u equals cosine of fifteen degrees, zero point nine six five nine. D two is minus zero point three five two. Negative. P of twenty-four, two fails. One of its eigenvalues is slightly above two root two.

19. So between n equals twenty-three and n equals twenty-four, at k equals two, the Ramanujan property switches off, and it stays off for every larger n. The next section explains why it stays off.

## E06S07 — The band at u = 1

20. Look at D k at the right end, u equals one. Cosine t equals one means t equals zero, and then cosine k t is also one, so T k of one is one for every k. A is two plus two, four. P is two times two, four. D k of one is seven plus four squared minus eight times four squared: one hundred twenty-one minus one hundred twenty-eight, which is minus seven. Negative, for every single k.

21. So just below u equals one there is a band where D k is negative. Call the largest root of D k below one u k; the band is from u k to one. Any grid point that lands in it makes the graph fail. And the grid point closest to one is j equals one, at u equals cosine of two pi over n. As n grows, two pi over n shrinks, and cosine of it rises toward one. Eventually it enters the band. Once it has entered, it never leaves, because it only keeps rising.

22. This gives a ceiling on n for each k. The graph fails as soon as cosine two pi over n exceeds u k, that is, as soon as n exceeds two pi divided by the arccosine of u k. Call that number B k. For k equals two, the largest root of D two below one is zero point nine six four one, and B two works out to twenty-three point three seven. So n at most twenty-three, matching what we found: twenty-three passes and twenty-four fails. For k equals three, B three is thirty-two point five, and for k equals four it is forty-two point zero. In each case the actual last Ramanujan n is exactly the whole-number part: twenty-three, thirty-two, forty-two.

23. Now the other end, u equals minus one. Cosine t equals minus one means t equals one hundred eighty degrees, and cosine k t is then minus one to the k: minus one when k is odd, plus one when k is even. Work it out: for odd k, A is minus four, P is four, D is seven plus four squared minus eight times sixteen, which is minus seven. For even k, A is zero, P is minus four, D is seven minus four squared minus zero, which is nine. So for odd k there is a second forbidden band at u equals minus one, and for even k there is not. Remember this. It will produce a law about which n survive for odd k.

## E06S08 — Recap

24. What you should now be able to do. Explain why squaring an inequality is only safe when both sides are nonnegative, and carry out the two checks in this episode. Derive two root two absolute A at most seven plus P from the eigenvalue formula. Explain why cosine k t is a polynomial in cosine t, and write T two and T three. Define D k of u, expand D two, and evaluate it at u equals cosine seventy-two to get twenty-eight. Show that D k of one is minus seven, and explain why that caps n for each k. And compute D k of minus one for odd and even k.

25. Next episode: a change of variable that makes k disappear from the geometry entirely, then two arguments, one with a famous inequality and one with a theorem about fractions, that make the whole family finite: no Ramanujan P of n, k exists with n above two hundred thirty, for any k whatsoever.
