# Episode 13 — Tiles: from each n to every n

Audience: knows the monodromy and certification from episodes ten and twelve. The tiling
idea is built with a postage-stamp example.

## E13S01 — The problem with one n at a time

1. Episode twelve certified Z of P of n, three equals eight for n from seventeen to thirty-three, each one separately. Krishnan's conjecture asks for every n at least thirteen. Certifying n one at a time can never get there: there are infinitely many n. We need a construction that, from finitely many certified pieces, produces a certificate for every n.

2. The clue is in how the monodromy is built. T is the product of n single-step matrices, one for each position around the ring. Each step matrix depends on the entries near its position. If the ring could be assembled from pieces, each piece contributing the identity to the product, then the whole product would be the identity, and different combinations of pieces would give different n. Identity times identity is identity, no matter how many factors.

## E13S02 — Why pieces interact, and the docking pattern

3. There is an obstacle. Each step matrix sees a window of entries of width about two k plus two around its position. Near the boundary between two pieces, the window straddles both. So the product over one piece depends on the entries of its neighbour, and the product does not split cleanly into one factor per piece. Pieces interact across their boundaries.

4. The fix is a docking pattern. Fix once and for all the values of the five kinds of entries, a, b, c, d, e, at k plus one consecutive positions. Call it the dock. A tile is a stretch of entries whose first k plus one positions and last k plus one positions both carry the dock, and whose interior positions are free. When two tiles are placed side by side, the end of one and the start of the next together form a stretch of two k plus two dock positions, and that stretch is the same no matter which two tiles meet.

5. Now look at any window of width two k plus two. Either it lies within one tile's interior plus docks, or it straddles a boundary. If it straddles, everything it sees is dock entries plus one tile's interior, because the docks are long enough that the window cannot reach the interior of the other tile. So the product of step matrices over a tile depends only on that tile's interior, never on its neighbours. The monodromy of a ring assembled from tiles is the product of the tile products. The project checked this numerically before relying on it: change one tile's interior, and the other tiles' products are unchanged to the last digit.

## E13S03 — The tiling theorem, with a postage-stamp example

6. Call a tile an identity tile if its product is the identity matrix, and all the entries required to be nonzero are nonzero. Here is the tiling theorem. Fix k and a length L. Suppose there is an identity tile of every length from L up to two L minus one. Then for every n at least L, there is a matrix on the P of n, k pattern with monodromy the identity, hence nullity two k plus two, hence Z of P of n, k equals M of P of n, k equals two k plus two.

7. The proof is the postage-stamp argument. If n is below two L, there is a single identity tile of length n; use it. If n is at least two L, peel off one tile of length L; what remains is n minus L, which is at least L, so recurse. Eventually the remainder is below two L and a single tile finishes. Concretely, with L equals twenty-one: n equals thirty-five is one tile of length thirty-five. n equals fifty-five is twenty-one plus thirty-four. n equals one hundred is twenty-one plus twenty-one plus twenty-one plus thirty-seven. n equals one hundred sixty-three is six tiles of length twenty-one plus one of length thirty-seven. Every n at least twenty-one is reachable, with no gaps and no conditions on n.

8. Why the whole interval from L to two L minus one, and not just a couple of lengths? Two lengths with no common factor, say twenty-one and twenty-two, generate all sufficiently large n as sums, but miss many small ones: the largest unreachable n is twenty times twenty-one minus twenty-one minus twenty-two, that is, four hundred nineteen. The interval guarantees there is nothing missing from L onward.

9. Pause on what this achieves. The statement for every n at least L has become a finite list of finite problems: find one identity tile for each of the L lengths from L to two L minus one, and certify each one with the method of episode twelve. Infinitely many theorems from finitely many certificates.

## E13S04 — Do identity tiles exist? Counting parameters

10. Whether identity tiles exist is a question about counting. A tile of length ell has ell minus two k plus two interior positions, each with five free entries: that is five times ell minus two k plus two unknowns. The identity condition is a two k plus two by two k plus two matrix equation: two k plus two squared equations. At k equals three, that is sixty-four equations. For the count of unknowns to reach sixty-four you need five times ell minus eight at least sixty-four, so ell minus eight at least thirteen, so ell at least twenty-one. At length twenty: sixty unknowns, sixty-four equations, too few. At twenty-one: sixty-five unknowns, enough.

11. And that is what happens. Below length twenty-one, Newton stalls, with residuals stuck around zero point four, and no identity tile is found. At twenty-one and every length above, identity tiles are found. For k equals three, tiles exist at every length from twenty-one to forty-three, which covers the required interval from twenty-one to forty-one with two to spare. Parameter counting predicted the threshold exactly.

## E13S05 — Certifying a tile

12. Each tile must be certified, and here the ceiling theorem from episode ten gives a gift. The condition T tile equals identity is a nasty rational equation. But consider the one-tile ring: put a single tile of length ell on P of ell, k, with the dock at the ends wrapping around to meet itself. Its monodromy is exactly the tile product. So T tile equals identity if and only if the one-tile matrix on P of ell, k has nullity two k plus two. And that is exactly the bilinear nullity system from episode twelve: A times K equals zero, with the affine Jacobian and the exact fractions. The dock entries are pinned at their exact whole-number values during certification, so certified tiles still fit together.

13. All twenty-three tiles at k equals three pass, with contraction constant at most two point two times ten to the minus ten. All thirty-three tiles at k equals four pass, covering lengths twenty-nine to sixty-one. At k equals five, twenty-one tiles pass, lengths fifty-four to seventy-five with two gaps, which is not a full interval but still generates every n from one hundred sixty-two upward as sums. Seventy-seven tile certifications in all, plus seventeen single-n certifications, every one evaluated in exact rational arithmetic.

## E13S06 — The theorems, and the complete rows

14. Put it together. Z of P of n, three equals M of P of n, three equals eight for every n at least seventeen. Z of P of n, four equals M of P of n, four equals ten for every n at least twenty-nine. Z of P of n, five equals M of P of n, five equals twelve for every n at least one hundred sixty-two. No divisibility conditions. Primes included.

15. Below those thresholds, an exhaustive search settles each value: for each candidate size, check every set of that size, playing the forcing game. The search uses the rotation to assume a minimum forcing set contains a fixed vertex, which cuts the work by a factor of n without losing exactness. At n equals twenty-eight, k equals four, ruling out every nine-vertex set took about one point two billion candidates. Before being trusted on any new value, the solver reproduced every published value, including Krishnan's corrected table.

16. The result is the complete determination of Z for k equals two, three, and four. For k equals two, from n equals five: five, four, six, five, six, six, six, then six forever from twelve. For k equals three, from n equals seven: six, six, six, eight, seven, seven, then eight forever from thirteen. For k equals four, from n equals nine: six, six, seven, six, eight, eight, nine, eight, nine, then ten forever from eighteen. None of these rows is monotone. None is constant from the first time the bound is attained: k equals three hits eight at n equals ten and then drops to seven.

17. In particular, Z of P of n, three equals eight for every n at least thirteen, and thirteen is the best possible threshold, since the value is seven at eleven and twelve. That is Krishnan's Conjecture 5, exactly as stated, proved. The missing ingredient he named, a lower-bound proof valid for all large n, is the tiling theorem. The k equals four row is the first exact determination of Z at k equals four at all, and the first for an infinite family at any k at least four.

18. Attribution, precisely. The correction of the published theorem, showing Z of P of twelve, three is seven, is Krishnan's. The contribution of this project is the lower bound for all large n, and with it the proof of his conjecture, and the k equals four and five results. Say exactly that, and no more.

## E13S07 — Recap

19. What you should now be able to do. Explain why the monodromy is a product and why that suggests pieces. Explain why pieces interact and how the docking pattern makes each tile's product depend only on its own interior. State the tiling theorem and prove it by the postage-stamp recursion, with n equals one hundred as twenty-one plus twenty-one plus twenty-one plus thirty-seven. Explain why the whole interval from L to two L minus one is needed. Count parameters to predict that tiles at k equals three start at length twenty-one. Explain why certifying a tile reduces to a one-tile nullity certification. State the three theorems and the three complete rows. And state the attribution correctly.
